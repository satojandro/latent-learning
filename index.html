Prologue

Words Become Arrows

2013 to 2020. Based on word2vec (Mikolov et al., Google, 2013), Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (Kaplan et al., OpenAI, 2020) and Zoom In (Olah et al., 2020). Links to all three are at the end of the chapter.

Scene 1

What does a word mean, to a computer?

To a computer, a word is just a row of letters. "Cat" and "kitten" look nothing alike, and "cat" and "car" look almost the same. Spelling tells you very little about meaning.

The way out came from a 1957 idea by the linguist J. R. Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." You learn what a new word means by seeing where it turns up. If a word keeps appearing near "crown", "throne" and "palace", you can guess a lot about it without a dictionary.

So describe every word by its neighbours. Then words with similar neighbours get similar descriptions, and you can draw them as points on a map. Words that keep similar company land close together.

    Scene 3

    2013: the arrow that means something

    In 2013, a team at Google led by Tomas Mikolov released word2vec. It did the same thing as the toy above, on a far bigger scale: billions of words of news text, and a few hundred directions instead of two. (Word2vec learns by predicting a word's neighbours rather than by counting them, but researchers later showed the two approaches are closely related.)

    Then they noticed something nobody had designed. Take the point for king, subtract man, add woman, and you land right next to queen. The step from "man" to "woman" is an arrow, and the same arrow leads from "king" to "queen", from "uncle" to "aunt", from "actor" to "actress".

    Other arrows turned up too. One led from countries to their capitals, another from a verb to its past tense. Meaning wasn't just stored as points. Directions meant things. That single idea runs through every chapter of this book.

    Two fine-print details

    The famous example is one of the neatest. Many analogies work less cleanly, and the standard test quietly leaves out the three words you started with. Without that rule, the nearest word to king − man + woman is often just "king".

    The arrows learned our prejudices too. A 2016 paper showed the same arithmetic completed "man is to computer programmer as woman is to…" with "homemaker". The map reflects the company words keep in human writing, stereotypes included. It was an early warning that these systems absorb what's in their training text, good and bad.

    Scene 4

    From word maps to word machines

    Word2vec gave every word one fixed point. But "bank" means something different in "river bank" and "bank account". A fixed map can't tell them apart.

    In 2017, another Google team introduced the transformer, in a paper called Attention Is All You Need. A transformer reads a whole passage at once and builds a fresh arrow for every word in its context, so the two banks end up in different places. It's the design behind nearly every chatbot today, and the subject of Chapter 1.

    Between 2018 and 2020, OpenAI trained a series of transformers called GPT, GPT-2 and GPT-3, each far bigger than the last. All of them had a single job: read some text and predict the next word. And each one was surprisingly better than the one before.

    Scene 5

    2020: bigger is predictably better

    In 2020, a team at OpenAI led by Jared Kaplan measured exactly how much better. They trained hundreds of models of different sizes and found something rare in science: a near-perfect pattern. Every time you multiply the computing power by ten, the model's error drops by about the same fraction. That held across a huge range, from tiny models to the biggest they could build.

    This pattern is called a scaling law. It's the reason AI companies now spend billions on bigger models with confidence. They can predict roughly how good a model will be before they build it.

    Scene 6

    Grown, not built

    So by 2020, people knew the recipe: a transformer, a mountain of text, the goal of predicting the next word, and a rule that nudges billions of numbers whenever a guess is wrong. They could even predict how good the result would be.

    What they didn't know was what had grown inside. Nobody wrote the rules the model follows. They emerged during training, in a space of thousands of unlabelled directions.

    A small group of researchers led by Chris Olah had already started looking. They took models that recognise images and studied them neuron by neuron. They found neurons that detect curves, and saw how they connect. In their 2020 article Zoom In, they showed a "car detector" assembled from simpler parts: windows near the top, a car body in the middle, wheels at the bottom.

    They called these wiring patterns circuits, and they made a bold bet: that models are made of understandable parts, if only we look carefully enough.

    In 2021, that group began asking the same question of transformers, the models that read and write language. They published their findings as a running series of articles called the Transformer Circuits Thread. This book follows that series, one discovery at a time.

    The end of the prologue

    What we know, and what we don't

    Known: you can know a word by its company

    Describing words by their neighbours gives a map where similar meanings sit close together.

    Known: directions carry meaning

    Steps through the map, like man to woman or country to capital, mean the same thing wherever you take them.

    Known: scale brings predictable gains

    More compute, data and size reduce a model's error along a smooth, predictable curve.

    Unknown: what grows inside?

    In 2020, nobody could explain how a big language model actually produces its answers.

    Unknown: why does scaling work so well?

    The curve was measured, not explained. Why it holds, and how far it will keep holding, are still debated.

    Unknown: are models made of readable parts?

    Circuits had been found in image models. Whether language models could be read the same way was the open bet.

    Chapter 1

    The Clerks and the Case File

    Based on A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (Elhage, Nanda, Olsson et al., Anthropic, 2021)

    The Prologue ended with a bet: that language models are made of parts we can understand. In late 2021, the first big article in the Transformer Circuits series set out to test it. The researchers began with the simplest transformers they could build, ones with zero, one or two layers, and tried to explain exactly what each one does. To keep things simple, they also removed one kind of part, which we'll meet below.

    To follow along, picture a courthouse.

    Scene 1

    One case file per word

    When a transformer reads your sentence, every word gets its own case file. The files are lined up on a long table, in order. At first each file holds very little: which word it is, and where it sits in the sentence.

    Then clerks work through the files, one row of clerks per layer. Each clerk reads, then adds notes. Clerks almost never throw notes away, so the file keeps growing. Researchers call this growing file the residual stream.

    There are two kinds of clerk:

    Messengers look across at earlier files and copy useful notes into the one they're working on. Researchers call them attention heads. They are the only way information travels between words.

    Thinkers work on one file alone, mulling over what's already in it and adding conclusions. These are the MLP layers. They're the part this paper set aside to keep things simple.

    When the clerks are finished, someone reads the last file and uses it to guess the next word. Step through a sketch of that below.

    The case files for "Ms Okafor … thanked Ms"

    A sketch

    This is an illustration, not a real model's notes. Real notes aren't words: they're arrows in a space with thousands of directions, as in the Prologue. The structure is real, though: one file per word, messengers moving notes between files, thinkers working within a file, and the last file used for the guess.

    Scene 2

    Zero clerks: the one-word guesser

    Start with the simplest case: a transformer with no clerks at all. The last file holds only one note, the current word. So the model can only learn "after this word, that word usually comes next." Nothing earlier in the sentence can reach it.

    The paper showed that's exactly what a zero-layer transformer learns: a table of which word tends to follow which. Here's a real one, learned from a 25-sentence courtroom story. Pick a word to see what it guesses comes next.

      Notice that after "ms" it always says "reyes", because Ms Reyes is the only Ms in the story. It can't do any better, because it can't see the rest of your sentence. For that, it needs a messenger.

      Scene 3

      The messenger: where to look, and what to copy

      Give the model one layer of messengers. Now, while working on the last file, a messenger can look back at every earlier file and decide which ones are worth copying from.

      The paper's key insight was that each messenger does two separate jobs, and you can study each on its own:

      Where to look. The current file asks a question, such as "is there a name earlier on?". Every earlier file carries a label that says what it holds. Where question and label match, the messenger pays attention. Researchers call this the QK circuit, for "query" and "key".

      What to copy. Having looked, the messenger writes something into the current file. Often it simply copies the word it found, making that word more likely to come next. This is the OV circuit, for "output" and "value".

      A one-layer model is full of messengers like this. The paper found they learn patterns of the form "A … B → C": when you see A, look back for B, and predict C. One real example was "keep … in → mind" and "keep … at → bay".

      A bug that shows how it works

      Because "where to look" and "what to copy" are separate, these patterns get mixed up. A messenger that learned "keep … in → mind" and "keep … at → bay" also nudges the model towards "keep … in → bay" and "keep … at → mind". It can't remember which pairs belong together. The paper found exactly this kind of mistake, which was good evidence that the explanation was right.

      Scene 4

      Every clerk casts a vote

      Here's the other big insight. Because clerks only ever add notes, the final guess is simply the sum of every clerk's contribution. So you can take a model apart: switch one clerk off, and see what it was adding.

      Below is a tiny real model with two contributors. The one-word guesser from Scene 2 is the direct path. A messenger looks back for names and nouns and copies them forward. Its "where to look" and "what to copy" settings are a handful of numbers we set by hand, but the attention maths is the real thing.

      Try this: on the first example, switch the messenger off and the guess falls back to "reyes". Then try the second example, which has two unfamiliar names. The messenger finds both, but it can't tell which name went with "ms", so it splits its vote between them, and the old habit "reyes" sneaks into first place. A one-layer model can't do better. Fixing that takes a second layer.

      Scene 5

      Two layers: clerks who read each other's notes

      With two layers, something new becomes possible. A second-row messenger can read notes that a first-row messenger left behind, and use them to decide where to look. The paper called this composition.

      Here's how that fixes the mix-up above. A first-row messenger in every file writes down "the word before me was…". Now the file for "okafor" carries the note "the word before me was ms". When a second-row messenger working on a later "ms" asks "which file came right after an earlier ms?", that note answers it, and it copies "okafor".

      That two-clerk team is called an induction head. The paper found induction heads in its two-layer models, and suspected they mattered far more than their simple job suggests. Chapter 2 is the story of finding out how much.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: the case file is a shared workspace

      Each word's file collects notes from every layer, and the final guess is read from the last file.

      Known: messengers do two separable jobs

      Where to look and what to copy can be studied one at a time, and small models can be explained this way exactly.

      Known: contributions add up

      Because clerks add rather than overwrite, you can split a guess into each clerk's vote and switch clerks off to test them.

      Unknown: the thinkers

      The paper left out the MLP "thinker" layers, where much of a real model's work happens. They're much harder to read, for reasons Chapter 3 explains.

      Unknown: does this scale?

      Exact explanations worked for one and two layers. Real models have dozens of layers and thousands of clerks.

      Unknown: how much do induction heads matter?

      They were a neat find in tiny models. Whether they were central to big models was the next question.

      Chapter 2

      The Copycat Trick

      Based on In-context Learning and Induction Heads (Olsson, Elhage, Nanda et al., Anthropic, 2022)

      Scene 1

      Learning without changing

      Here's a puzzle. Once a model has finished training, its billions of numbers are frozen. Nothing about it changes while you talk to it. And yet it clearly learns from what you tell it.

      Lawyers do this with every contract. Write "Northwind Logistics Ltd (the 'Supplier')" on page one, and from then on a good reader knows exactly who "the Supplier" is. A language model does the same thing. Introduce a made-up name, a new term or an odd format, and a few lines later it's using it correctly.

      Researchers call this in-context learning: learning from the text in front of you, rather than from training. You can measure it. A model guesses the 500th word of a document much better than the 50th, because by then it has 450 more words of context to learn from.

      The 2022 paper asked: which clerks make this possible? Their answer was the induction heads we met at the end of Chapter 1.

      Scene 2

      The copycat rule

      An induction head follows one simple rule:

      If you've seen A followed by B before, then the next time you see A, guess B.

      That sounds almost too simple to matter. But it's exactly the trick you need for "the Supplier", for names, for repeated phrases, and for any pattern that appears earlier in the same text.

      The researchers' favourite test makes the point vividly. Take a list of random words, so there's no meaning or grammar to lean on, and repeat it. The first time through, nothing can predict what comes next. The second time through, a model with induction heads gets almost every word right.

      The repeated-nonsense test

      This toy runs the copycat rule exactly as written above: for each word, find where it appeared before, and guess whatever came after it. It isn't a trained model, but it's the same rule the paper found trained models carrying out. Real models aren't quite this perfect, but they show the same dramatic jump on the second pass.

      Scene 3

      Two clerks, one trick

      Remember the mix-up at the end of Chapter 1? In "mr osei and ms novak spoke then the judge thanked ms", a single messenger found both names but couldn't tell which one belonged to "ms".

      An induction head is a team of two clerks:

      Clerk 1, in an early row, works on every file and writes down one fact: "the word just before me was…". So the file for "novak" gets the note "before me: ms".

      Clerk 2, in a later row, works on the last file, "ms". It asks: "which earlier file has the note before me: ms?" That's the file for "novak", so it copies "novak" forward.

      Clerk 2 can only ask that question because Clerk 1 left the note. This is the composition from Chapter 1: one clerk's notes changing where another clerk looks.

      Both settings also use the one-word guesser from Chapter 1. When nothing earlier matches, the induction team looks at the "start" position, which copies nothing. Real models often use the first position as this kind of "look nowhere" option. Try this: switch between the two clerks on the first example, then type a sentence of your own that repeats a pair of words.

      Scene 4

      The bump in the curve

      Here's what made the paper famous. When you train a model, its error usually falls smoothly. But the researchers spotted a small bump early in training, where progress stalls and then lurches forward.

      When they looked closely, three things happened at the same moment, in model after model:

      1. Induction heads suddenly formed. 2. The model's in-context learning jumped: it got much better at using earlier text. 3. The bump appeared in the training curve.

      And models with only one layer, which can't build the two-clerk team, showed no bump and no jump. Scrub through training below to see the pattern.

      A model's training, replayed

      Scene 5

      More than copying

      In bigger models, the researchers found induction heads doing looser versions of the trick. Instead of "A followed by B", it becomes "something like A, followed by something like B". Some heads helped with translation, lining up a word in one language with its partner in another. Others continued patterns that were similar in meaning rather than identical in spelling.

      That led to the paper's bold suggestion: induction heads might be the mechanism behind most of the in-context learning in large models, not just the literal copying.

      How strong is the evidence?

      The authors were careful to grade their own claims. In small models with no thinker layers, the evidence was causal: switch the induction heads off, and in-context learning largely disappears. They even changed the architecture so a one-layer model could form induction heads, and the bump moved with them.

      In large models, the evidence was mostly correlational: induction heads and in-context learning arrive together, but that doesn't prove one causes the other. As a lawyer might put it, a strong circumstantial case, not a confession.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: models learn from context

      Without changing their weights, models get better the more of a document they read.

      Known: induction heads copy patterns

      A two-clerk team carries out "A then B, so after A guess B", using one clerk's notes to direct another's attention.

      Known: they arrive suddenly

      Induction heads, better in-context learning and a bump in the training curve appear together, early in training.

      Unknown: is this most of it?

      Whether induction heads explain most in-context learning in large models was a well-argued hypothesis, not a proof.

      Unknown: what about the thinkers?

      These results were cleanest in models without MLP "thinker" layers. Real models are full of them.

      Unknown: why are the thinkers so hard to read?

      Their neurons kept responding to unrelated things at once. Explaining that puzzle is the next chapter.

      Chapter 3

      The Crowded Office

      Based on Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage et al., Anthropic, 2022)

      Here's a reminder of the characters you'll meet in every chapter. Inside a language model, every idea it can think about is stored as an arrow: a direction in a vast space. The space is the model's private filing system. Researchers call it latent space, which just means "hidden space", because nobody labelled any of its directions.

      The case file

      What the model is thinking about right now. It's passed from clerk to clerk as the model reads your words.

      The clerks

      The model's layers. Each one reads the file, adds a few notes, and passes it on.

      The concepts

      Ideas like "French", "a legal term" or "octopus". There are millions of them.

      The desks

      The directions available to store concepts in. There are only a few thousand.

      Scene 1

      The neuron that couldn't make up its mind

      Early researchers had a hopeful plan. A model is made of tiny parts called neurons. If each neuron stood for one idea, you could read a model like a dictionary: here's the "French" neuron, here's the "angry" neuron.

      Sometimes it worked. But again and again, they found neurons like this one, which lights up for things that have nothing to do with each other.

      Meet one jumbled neuron

      Try it

      This pattern is a real one: a 2023 Anthropic study found a single neuron in a small language model that responded to academic citations, English dialogue, web requests and Korean text. Researchers call neurons like this polysemantic, meaning "many meanings". For years, they were one of the field's biggest puzzles.

      Scene 2

      Too many people, too few desks

      Here's the problem. A model knows about millions of concepts, but it only has a few thousand directions to store them in. That's like an office with a thousand employees and a dozen desks.

      Offices solve this with hot-desking. If most people only come in occasionally, they can share. The rarer the visits, the less often two people want the same desk on the same day.

      Below, twelve people share four desks. Drag the slider to change how often each of them comes in, and watch how often the office overflows.

      When everyone comes in every day, the office overflows constantly. At once a month, overflow almost never happens. A model faces the same trade-off. Most concepts are rare (you can read a lot of text before anyone mentions octopus anatomy), so they can share space and rarely get in each other's way.

      Scene 3

      A room that's much bigger than it looks

      For a model, a "desk" is a direction. Two concepts stay completely out of each other's way when their arrows are at a right angle. On a flat page, you can only fit two arrows at right angles: one across, one up.

      But a model's space doesn't have two directions. It has thousands. And in a space like that, something strange happens: if you pick arrows completely at random, almost every pair ends up nearly at a right angle. There's room for vastly more "almost separate" concepts than there are directions.

      Don't take my word for it. The chart below really does pick 150 random arrows and measures the angle between every pair.

      Angles between random arrows

      Real calculation

      In 2 directions, the angles are spread out evenly, and arrows constantly overlap. By 1,000 directions, nearly every pair is within a few degrees of a right angle. Real models have thousands of directions. Our flat-page intuition badly underestimates how roomy that is.

      Scene 4

      Watch a real tiny model learn

      This is the experiment at the heart of the paper. The researchers built the smallest possible model: it has to store 5 concepts in a space with only 2 directions. Then they asked what it does when the concepts are common, and what it does when they're rare.

      The model below isn't an animation. It's a real neural network training live in your browser, using the same setup. Each coloured arrow is where it chose to store one concept. Concept 1 matters most to its score, concept 5 matters least.

      5 concepts, 2 directions

      Training…

      Try this: start at "every time" and wait a few seconds. Then slide towards "very rarely", one notch at a time, and watch the arrows rearrange themselves.

      Here's what you should see, and it's what the researchers saw.

      When concepts are common, the model behaves like a cautious filing clerk. It keeps only the two most important concepts, gives each a private direction at a right angle, and simply gives up on the other three. Sharing would cause too many clashes.

      As concepts get rarer, the model starts squeezing more in. First three. Then four, arranged as two pairs pointing in opposite directions. Finally all five, spread into a perfect pentagon. Nobody told it to make a pentagon. It found that shape on its own, because it's the fairest way to give five rare concepts as much room as possible.

      That squeezing is superposition: more concepts than directions, sharing the space because they seldom appear at the same time.

      Scene 5

      Ignoring the whispers

      Sharing has a cost. When concepts squeeze together, each one leaks a little into its neighbours. Look at the pentagon: the arrows aren't at right angles, so whenever one concept is active, its neighbours get a faint, false signal too.

      The model handles this with a simple rule: ignore anything quieter than a certain level. Faint leakage gets thrown away, and only the strong, real signal gets through. This works only because the concepts are rare. If they all showed up at once, the leaks would pile up into a roar.

      Two more findings

      Concepts that appear together get kept apart. "Lawyer" and "court" turn up in the same sentences all the time, so sharing would mean constant clashes. Models tend to give such pairs separate directions.

      Opposites make perfect desk-mates. Concepts that never appear together can point in exactly opposite directions, like the two ends of a see-saw. Only one can be up at a time. You may have spotted these pairs in the toy model above.

      And now the jumbled neuron from Scene 1 makes sense. A neuron is a single, fixed spot in the model. When many concepts share the space, a single neuron catches a little of several of them. It isn't confused. It's a shared desk.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: important, common ideas get their own direction

      When space is tight, models prioritise. The ideas that matter most and come up most often get private directions.

      Known: rare ideas share space

      Superposition lets a model store far more concepts than it has directions, as long as those concepts seldom appear together.

      Known: it makes neat shapes on its own

      In toy models, shared concepts settle into pairs, triangles, pentagons and other tidy geometric arrangements.

      Unknown: how much of this happens in big models?

      The paper studied tiny toy models. Whether giant models use superposition in exactly the same way was an open question in 2022.

      Unknown: can we untangle it?

      If concepts are squeezed together, reading a model means pulling them back apart. In 2022, nobody had shown how to do that at scale.

      Unknown: what does the leakage cost?

      Researchers suspect that some model mistakes come from concepts leaking into each other at the wrong moment. That's still a hypothesis.

      In 2023, researchers built a tool that finds the shared desks and separates the concepts sitting at them. For the first time, they could read thousands of clean, single-meaning concepts out of a real model. Read Chapter 4 ↓

      Chapter 4

      Untangling the Desks

      Based on Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning (Bricken, Templeton, Batson, Chen, Jermyn et al., Anthropic, 2023)

      Scene 1

      The detective's problem

      Chapter 3 left us with a crowded office. Concepts share desks, so each neuron responds to a jumble of unrelated things. Remember the neuron that fired for citations, dialogue, web requests and Korean? It was found in this very study.

      The researchers now faced a detective's problem. They could see how busy each desk was at every moment: the neuron readings. What they wanted was the roster: who was actually sitting there.

      A court faces the same problem when reconstructing events. The evidence is incomplete, and many stories could fit it. A sensible judge prefers the account that explains all the evidence with the fewest moving parts. That single principle turns out to be the key.

      Scene 2

      Explain every day with as few people as possible

      Their tool is called dictionary learning, carried out with a sparse autoencoder. It works like a translator with a strict rule:

      1. Expand. Take the neuron readings and translate them into a much longer list of possible concepts, the "dictionary". The model in the paper had 512 neurons, and the dictionary had thousands of entries.

      2. Be stingy. For each moment, use as few dictionary entries as possible. Most entries must stay silent. That's what "sparse" means.

      3. Check your work. Translate back, and make sure the result matches the original readings.

      Train that translator on millions of moments, and each dictionary entry tends to settle on one real concept. Researchers call the entries features.

      You can try it on the crowded office from Chapter 3. Below, five hidden concepts share two desks, arranged in the pentagon you saw the model invent. The autoencoder only sees the dots, which are the desk readings at different moments. It has to discover where the five concepts are.

      Scene 3

      Neurons versus features

      Now compare the two ways of reading the office. Below, each of the five concepts is switched on by itself, and we measure what responds: the two desks (the neurons), and the dictionary entries the autoencoder just learned (the features).

      Who responds to each concept?

      Live from the autoencoder above

      Each neuron responds to several concepts at once: that's the jumble. Once the autoencoder has settled, each feature responds to just one. That's what the paper's title means. Monosemantic means "one meaning", the opposite of the polysemantic neurons from Chapter 3.

      Scene 4

      What they found in a real model

      The researchers ran this on a small real language model, with one layer and 512 neurons in its thinker layer. They trained dictionaries of many sizes. In the run they studied most closely, the 512 neurons unpacked into about 4,000 features.

      Many were strikingly clear. One feature responded to Arabic script, another to DNA sequences, another to base64 (a way of encoding computer data as letters and numbers), another to Hebrew. When people were asked to judge how interpretable features and neurons were, the features won by a wide margin.

      Three more findings made the result convincing:

      They're real causes, not just labels. Artificially switching a feature on changes what the model writes. Turn up the base64 feature, and the model starts producing base64 text.

      They show up again and again. Train a second model from scratch, and very similar features appear in it. The researchers called this universality.

      The features explain most of what the model does. Swapping the neuron readings for the autoencoder's translation kept most of the model's performance, though not all of it.

      The fine print

      The translation isn't perfect. Some of what the model does slips through the dictionary unexplained, and some features are hard to put into words. And this was a tiny one-layer model. Whether the method would work on a big, modern model was the obvious open question.

      Scene 5

      Zooming in and out

      One more surprise. The size of the dictionary changes what you find. A small dictionary gives broad concepts. A bigger one splits them into finer ones, like zooming in on a map. The paper called this feature splitting, and saw whole families of related features appear as dictionaries grew.

      It's a lot like how a body of law gets organised. Here's an invented example to show the shape of the idea. It's an illustration, not a result from the paper.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: the jumble can be untangled

      A sparse autoencoder can unpack polysemantic neurons into many features that each mean one thing.

      Known: features are real and recurring

      Switching a feature on changes the model's behaviour, and similar features appear in separately trained models.

      Known: the zoom level is a choice

      Bigger dictionaries split broad features into finer ones, in families.

      Unknown: does it work on big models?

      This was a one-layer model with 512 neurons. Modern models are thousands of times larger.

      Unknown: what slips through?

      Part of the model's behaviour isn't captured by the features. Nobody yet knew how much that leftover mattered.

      Unknown: how do features connect?

      Finding the concepts is step one. Seeing how they combine into reasoning is a separate, harder step.
