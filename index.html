Prologue

Words Become Arrows

2013 to 2020. Based on two 2013 papers by Tomas Mikolov and colleagues (the word-analogy paper and word2vec), Bolukbasi et al. on bias in word maps (2016), Kaplan et al., Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (OpenAI, 2020) and Olah et al., Zoom In (2020). Every source is linked at the end of the chapter.

This book is about a strange situation. People have built machines that write essays, pass exams and argue about poetry, and nobody can fully say how they do it. The machines weren't programmed with rules. They were grown, and the rules grew inside them.

The story of reading those rules starts with a small, odd discovery about words, made in 2013. It ends, seven years later, with two bets: one that bigger machines would keep getting better at a predictable rate, and one that their insides could be read like a book. Both bets set up everything that follows.

Scene 1

What does a word mean, to a computer?

To a computer, a word is just a row of letters. "Cat" and "kitten" look nothing alike, and "cat" and "car" look almost the same. Spelling tells you very little about meaning.

For decades, most language software simply gave each word a number in a list. Word 4,071 might be "cat" and word 4,072 "catalogue". The numbers carried no meaning at all. As the word2vec paper put it in 2013, these systems treated words "as atomic units", with "no notion of similarity between words". Learning something about "cat" taught the system nothing about "kitten".

The way out came from a 1957 line by the linguist J. R. Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." You learn what a new word means by seeing where it turns up. If a word keeps appearing near "crown", "throne" and "palace", you can guess a lot about it without a dictionary.

So describe every word by its neighbours. Words with similar neighbours get similar descriptions, and you can draw them as points on a map. Words that keep similar company land close together.

    Go deeper: what the toy actually computes Level 2

    Step 1, counting. For each of our four words, the toy counts how often each other word shares a sentence with it, after dropping filler like "the" and "of". That gives every word a long row of counts, one column per neighbour.

    Step 2, surprise. Raw counts overrate common neighbours. So each count is replaced by its pointwise mutual information: how much more often the two words meet than you'd expect if they were scattered at random.

    PMI(word, neighbour) = log [ P(word and neighbour together) ÷ ( P(word) × P(neighbour) ) ]

    In plain words: if "crown" turns up with "king" four times as often as chance predicts, the score is log 4. Negative scores (meeting less than chance) are set to zero, which makes it positive PMI, or PPMI.

    Step 3, squeezing. Each word now has 22 numbers. To draw them, the toy finds the two directions along which the four words differ most, using principal component analysis, and plots each word's position along those two. With every neighbour visible, the first direction holds 80% of the spread between the words and lines up with royalty. The second holds 20% and lines up with gender. Together they keep over 99% of it, so this flat picture loses almost nothing.

    Notice what nobody did. No step mentions royalty or gender. The two directions fall out of the counting. That is the whole trick of this chapter, in miniature.

    Scene 3

    2013: the arrow that means something

    In 2013, Tomas Mikolov, then working with Wen-tau Yih and Geoffrey Zweig at Microsoft Research, looked inside a language model that had been trained on 320 million words of broadcast news. Its only job was to guess the next word. Along the way it had given every word a list of numbers, a point in a space of up to 1,600 directions.

    They found something nobody had designed. Take the point for king, subtract man, add woman, and you land very close to queen. The step from "man" to "woman" is an arrow, and roughly the same arrow leads from "king" to "queen". In their words, each relationship "is characterized by a relation-specific vector offset". Plural nouns had their own arrow (apple to apples, car to cars). So did past tenses and comparatives.

    "The model itself has no knowledge of syntax or morphology or semantics. Remarkably, training such a purely lexical model to maximize likelihood will induce word representations with striking syntactic and semantic properties."Mikolov, Yih & Zweig, 2013, §3

    The same year, Mikolov moved to Google and, with Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeffrey Dean, released word2vec. Its insight was to throw away most of the network. Instead of a full language model, it trained a very simple one: given a word, guess its neighbours (or given the neighbours, guess the word). That was cheap enough to run on about 6 billion words of Google News. The arrows got sharper. Paris − France + Italy landed on Rome.

    Meaning wasn't just stored as points. Directions meant things. That single idea runs through every chapter of this book.

    Go deeper: how good were the arrows, really? Level 2

    In the papers: Mikolov, Yih & Zweig, §5 The Vector Offset Method and §6 Experimental Results; word2vec, §4.1 Task Description and §4.4 Large Scale Parallel Training of Models.

    To answer "a is to b as c is to ?", you take the arrow from a to b, add it to c, and look for the nearest word. The 2013 paper writes it like this:

    y = xb − xa + xc ,   answer = the word w whose xw points most nearly the same way as y

    In plain words: walk the arrow, then pick the word whose own arrow points most nearly in the same direction as where you ended up. "Nearest" here is measured by angle (cosine similarity), not distance.

    The scores. On 8,000 grammar questions (good is to better as rough is to ?), the best 2013 recurrent model got 39.6% exactly right. Guessing gets a small fraction of one percent. Word2vec's own test had 8,869 meaning questions (Athens is to Greece as Oslo is to ?) and 10,675 grammar questions. Its best model, the "skip-gram" version with 1,000 dimensions trained on 6 billion words, got 65.6% overall. Synonyms counted as wrong, so the real quality is a bit higher than the score.

    Two tricks that help. Averaging the arrow over ten example pairs instead of one improved accuracy by about 10 percentage points. And bigger maps need more text: Table 2 of the word2vec paper shows that adding dimensions without adding data, or data without dimensions, soon stops helping. Hold that thought for Scene 7.

    Deeper still: the fine print on king − man + woman Level 3

    The test quietly removes the starting words. Word2vec's paper says so in one parenthesis: "we discard the input question words during this search". That matters. A 2016 paper (Bolukbasi et al., Appendix A) points out that for many questions, the nearest word to the landing point is the word you started from. Without the rule, king − man + woman often lands nearest to "king" itself. The arrow moves you toward queen, but not always past king.

    The famous example is one of the neatest. Grammar arrows (plural, past tense) tend to be cleaner than arrows for facts. So when you see "king − man + woman = queen", read it as "the step from man to woman points roughly the same way as the step from king to queen". That's still remarkable. It just isn't magic.

    Why directions are so powerful. If "lawyer", "cross-examining", "robot" and "moon" each have a direction, the model can represent a lawyer cross-examining a robot on the moon by combining them, even if no text ever described that scene. A list of word numbers can't do that. A space of meaningful directions can, and later chapters show that language models lean on this heavily.

    Scene 4

    The arrows learned our prejudices

    In 2016, Tolga Bolukbasi, Kai-Wei Chang, James Zou, Venkatesh Saligrama and Adam Kalai took the most popular public word2vec map, 300 directions trained on Google News, and asked it to complete analogies. Their paper's title gives away what came back: Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker? Ask it "he is to doctor as she is to ?" and it answered "nurse".

    They found that gender wasn't scattered randomly through the map. It was largely one direction, roughly the arrow from "he" to "she". Lay every job along that arrow and the extremes on the "she" end were homemaker, nurse, receptionist, librarian and socialite. On the "he" end: maestro, skipper, protégé, philosopher and captain. A map made from the company words keep in the news had absorbed the company that jobs keep with pronouns.

    Then came the hopeful part. If bias is a direction, you can measure it, and you can take it out.

    Take the gender direction out

    Hand-placed words, real projection

    The positions are set by hand to echo the paper's lists. The arithmetic is real: the gender arrow is computed from "he" and "she", and pressing the button subtracts each job word's shadow along that arrow, exactly as the paper's "neutralise" step does in 300 dimensions. Words that are gendered by definition, like king and queen, are left alone. Look at what survives, too: the jobs still sit in two clumps up and down the map.

    Go deeper: direct bias, indirect bias, and a lawyer's vocabulary Level 2

    In the paper: §4 Gender stereotypes in word embeddings, §5 Geometry of Gender and Bias, §6 Debiasing algorithms.

    The authors borrowed two terms from discrimination law. Direct bias is a neutral word leaning toward one gender, like "receptionist" sitting closer to "she" than to "he". Indirect bias is subtler: two neutral words pulled together because both lean the same way. Their example: on the arrow from "football" to "softball", the jobs nearest the softball end included bookkeeper, receptionist and waitress. Neither sport nor job is gendered by definition. The pull comes through gender anyway, the way a hiring rule can discriminate through a proxy without ever mentioning sex.

    Did people agree? They asked ten US crowd workers per item. Of 150 analogies the map generated for the she–he arrow, 29 (19%) were judged stereotyped by a majority. After their "hard debiasing", which neutralises job words and makes pairs like grandmother and grandfather equally far from every neutral word, only 6% were. "He is to doctor as she is to ?" now answered "physician". Standard accuracy tests barely moved.

    "Surprisingly, not only does the embedding capture bias, but it also contains sufficient information to reduce this bias."Bolukbasi et al., 2016, §1

    A later warning. In 2019, Hila Gonen and Yoav Goldberg showed that this kind of debiasing mostly hides bias. After the gender arrow is removed, words that used to lean "she" still cluster together, and a simple classifier can still sort them. That's the second clump in the toy above. Removing one direction doesn't remove everything that direction was correlated with.

    Keep this scene in mind for Chapter 5. "A concept is a direction, and you can turn it down" will come back, in a much bigger model, as a dial.

    Scene 5

    From word maps to word machines

    Word2vec gave every word one fixed point. But "bank" means something different in "river bank" and "bank account". A fixed map can't tell them apart.

    In 2017, a Google team introduced the transformer, in a paper called Attention Is All You Need. A transformer reads a whole passage and builds a fresh arrow for every word in its context, so the two banks end up in different places. It's the design behind nearly every chatbot today, and the subject of Chapter 1.

    Between 2018 and 2020, OpenAI trained a series of transformers called GPT, GPT-2 and GPT-3, each far bigger than the last. GPT-2 had 1.5 billion numbers to tune; GPT-3 had 175 billion. All of them had a single job: read some text and predict the next word.

    That job sounds shallow. It isn't. Try being the model.

    Be the model: predict the next word

    The evidence locker had one key. Mr Chen reported his copy lost on Monday. Dr Patel spent the whole week at a conference in Geneva. On Thursday, Ms Reyes signed the key out at nine and returned it at ten. At ten past nine, the guard heard the locker door. The person who opened it was ___

    Ms Reyes. To get one word right you had to track three people, one key, a city and a timeline, and rule two people out. A model that gets this kind of word right, across millions of stories, is being pushed to keep track of who did what. That's why "predict the next word" turned out to be such a demanding teacher. How much real understanding it produces is one of the questions this book keeps returning to.

    Go deeper: the dials and the case file Level 2

    Two kinds of numbers live inside a model, and it helps to keep them apart from the start.

    Weights: the dials

    Billions of numbers set during training, then frozen. They are the clerks' trained habits, the "laws of physics" of the model. GPT-3's 175 billion are weights. Every conversation uses the same ones.

    Activations: the case file

    The numbers the model computes while reading your text, one bundle per word. They're the objects moving through those laws. They exist for this conversation only. Chapter 1 calls each word's bundle its case file.

    The word arrows from Scene 3 are activations of a very simple kind. In a transformer, every word's arrow is rebuilt layer by layer as it reads. The width of that arrow, the number of directions available, is what the 2020 scaling paper calls dmodel, "the dimension of the residual stream". In this book, those directions are the desks.

    A measured reason the transformer won. The scaling paper compared transformers with the older "LSTM" design. LSTMs did as well on the first words of a passage but stopped improving after fewer than 100 words of context. Transformers kept getting better at predicting words right up to the 1,024th. They use long context far better (Kaplan et al., §3.2.1, Figure 7).

    Scene 6

    Grown more than built

    By 2020 people knew the recipe: a transformer, a mountain of text, the goal of predicting the next word, and a rule that nudges every dial a tiny amount whenever a guess is wrong. Repeat a few hundred thousand times.

    Engineers design all of those conditions. They don't design what results. They're more like gardeners than architects: they choose the seed, the soil and the trellis, and then the plant grows. We know how to grow it. We don't know what grows.

    Here is the puzzle. The nudging rule is blind. It never sees a concept. Each nudge only says "turn these dials a little so this one guess would have been better". So why would anything orderly, like a "plural" direction, come out of billions of blind nudges? Run it and see.

    Predict before you press

    In the toy below, each example nudges one shared "plural" dial by 0.2, and also nudges a random one-off quirk dial by a full 1.0, up or down. The quirk nudges are five times bigger. After 1,000 examples, which dial is biggest?

Each quirk dial gets about a hundred of them, and a hundred random ±1 steps usually leave you only about ten steps from where you began. Press the buttons and check.

    Why blind nudges build structure

    Real calculation, simplified picture

    After one example a quirk wins, because its nudge is bigger. After hundreds, the shared dial towers over the rest, because a pattern that helps millions of predictions gets pushed the same way every time, and one-off accidents don't. Real training is vastly more complicated. This is the core of why it produces structure at all: structure wins because it's reusable.

    Order without an orderer

    This isn't the only place where simple local rules produce structure that nobody designed.

    The common law. No legislator drafted the doctrine of negligence. Judges decided individual cases, and the principles that kept working in case after case survived and hardened into law.

    Bitcoin. A short list of rules, followed by strangers who don't trust each other, keeps a global ledger stable with nobody in charge of it.

    Evolution. One blunt rule, keep what reproduces, plus a very long time, produced the eye.

    Training a model belongs to this family. Which is exactly why its results need to be studied, rather than looked up.

    Go deeper: the world, seen through its shadows Level 2

    A language model never sees the world. It sees text. But text is a shadow of the world, cast by billions of people who did see it, and wrote down what happened, where and when. In a sense, people are the model's sensors. Even the scaling paper's training set was filtered by people: it was built from web pages linked on Reddit that at least three readers had upvoted.

    Can a model rebuild the world from its shadows? Two later studies, beyond this prologue's dates, give a striking partial answer.

    Othello (Li et al., 2022). A small transformer was shown only lists of moves from the board game Othello, like "C4, D3, E6", never a board or a rule. It learned to suggest legal moves, with an error rate of 0.01% (an untrained copy: 93%). It wasn't just memorising: trained on games with a quarter of all openings removed, it still made legal moves on those openings 99.98% of the time. Inside, the researchers found a representation of the board, and editing it changed the moves the model suggested. Their probe needed to be non-linear. A 2023 follow-up by Neel Nanda and colleagues found the board is stored as straight directions, once you describe squares as "mine" and "the opponent's" rather than "black" and "white".

    Space and time (Gurnee & Tegmark, 2023). In the Llama-2 family of models, simple straight-line probes could read off the latitude and longitude of places, and the dates of historical figures, artworks and news headlines, from the model's activations. They even found individual "space neurons" and "time neurons". The authors are careful: this is a map and a timeline, which they call "basic ingredients of a world model", not a full one.

    In the papers: Li et al., §2.3 Othello-GPT usually predicts legal moves and §4 Validating probes with interventional experiments; Gurnee & Tegmark, §3 Linear models of space and time and §5 Space and time neurons.

    Scene 7

    2020: bigger is predictably better

    In January 2020, a team at OpenAI led by Jared Kaplan and Sam McCandlish measured how much better bigger models get. They trained a wide range of transformers, from under a thousand to 1.5 billion tunable numbers, on 23 billion tokens (word pieces) of web text, and measured each one's error.

    They found something rare outside physics: three clean laws. Make the model bigger, give it more text, or spend more computing power, and the error falls along a straight line on a "times ten" chart. Each time you multiply one of them by ten, the error shrinks by the same fraction, as long as the other two aren't holding it back. Some of these trends held across more than seven orders of magnitude.

    Predict before you slide

    According to the paper, if you make a model ten times bigger (and give it enough data), how much lower does its error get?

    About 16%. Each tenfold jump in size multiplies the error by 10−0.076 ≈ 0.84. That sounds small, but it repeats: a thousand times bigger takes you to 0.84 × 0.84 × 0.84 ≈ 0.59 of the starting error. Pick "Parameters" in the chart and slide along to see it.

    The three scaling laws

    The paper's fitted formulas and ranges

    Each solid line is the paper's own fitted formula, drawn over the range it actually measured (from its Figure 1). Error here is the paper's measure, "loss" in nats per token: lower is better. The dashed part of the compute line is the formula extended beyond what was measured, out to the point where the paper says its own laws start to contradict each other. On a "times ten" scale the laws are straight lines. Press the button to see the same curve on an ordinary ruler scale, where nearly all the gain seems to happen at the very start.

    Go deeper: the formulas, and what "loss" means Level 2

    In the paper: §1.2 Summary of Scaling Laws, §3 Empirical Results and Basic Power Laws, Appendix A Summary of Power Laws.

    The paper writes the parameter law like this:

    L(N) = (Nc / N)αN,   αN ≈ 0.076,   Nc ≈ 8.8 × 1013

    In plain words: the loss L equals some big constant divided by the model size N, raised to a small power. The small power, 0.076, is the whole story. It's why doubling the model only trims the loss by 5% (2−0.076 ≈ 0.95), and why the gains never stop but never come fast. The data law has power 0.095, and the compute law 0.050. The paper says the constants like Nc "do not have a fundamental meaning": they depend on how the text is chopped into tokens.

    What a loss number feels like. Loss is the model's average surprise at the true next token. If you raise e (about 2.718) to the power of the loss, you get how many equally likely options the model is effectively torn between. The smallest models in the chart score about 6.8, which is like choosing blind among roughly 900 tokens. The biggest score about 2.3: like choosing among 10. That is the distance from babble to fluent prose.

    Shape barely matters. With the number of parameters held fixed, the ratio of depth to width could change forty-fold and the loss moved only a few percent. A 6-layer model with 4,288 desks came within 3% of a 48-layer model with 1,600 (§3.1, Figure 5). Size is what counts.

    And where do these laws come from? The authors don't know. In their caveats: "At present we do not have a solid theoretical understanding for any of our proposed scaling laws." They compare the laws to the ideal gas law, a reliable rule about a whole gas that holds regardless of what each molecule is doing, and hope for the 'statistical mechanics' underneath their 'thermodynamics'.

    Deeper still: how to spend a budget, and where the laws must break Level 3

    In the paper: §4 Charting the Infinite Data Limit and Overfitting, §6 Optimal Allocation of the Compute Budget, §6.3 Contradictions and a Conjecture.

    Big models learn faster per example. Larger models reach the same loss after seeing fewer tokens. So with a fixed budget, the paper's answer was: build a very big model and stop training it early, long before it has finished learning. For every tenfold increase in compute, make the model about 5 times bigger (N grows as C0.73) and the data only about 2 times bigger (D grows as C0.27). Pick "Compute" in the chart to see this recipe for each budget. The paper's line: "Big models may be more important than big data."

    To avoid overfitting, data should grow at least as N0.74: make the model 8 times bigger and you need only about 5 times more data.

    Later revised. In 2022, a DeepMind team (Hoffmann et al., the "Chinchilla" paper) redid the budget experiment with different training settings and found that model size and data should grow at about the same rate. By that measure many large models of the time had been trained on too little text. The power-law shape survived. The recipe changed.

    The laws contain their own expiry date. Followed far enough, the compute law predicts a loss lower than the data law allows for the small amount of data the recipe uses. The two lines cross at roughly 104 petaflop-days, 1012 parameters and 1012 tokens, at a loss of about 1.7 nats per token. The authors stress that these numbers are "highly uncertain", by an order of magnitude either way. They conjecture this might be roughly where the reliable information in natural language runs out. Nobody has settled it.

    A known known, and a known unknown

    The scaling laws let labs predict how well a model will do before building it. That's why companies began spending billions with confidence. But the laws predict one number, the average loss. They say nothing about what is inside, or which abilities appear at which size. The paper says so itself: "Smooth quantitative change can mask major qualitative improvements: 'more is different'." A smooth economy can hide the invention of the steam engine.

    Scene 8

    Zoom in

    So by 2020, people could predict how good a model would be. What they couldn't say was what had grown inside.

    A small group led by Chris Olah, then at OpenAI, had already started looking, in models that recognise images. In March 2020 they published Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits. It opens with the microscope. Before microscopes, biology studied whole animals. After them, it studied cells, and cellular biology "isn't just more careful zoology". Could neural networks have a cellular biology too? What if you studied one neuron, even one weight, at a time, for thousands of hours?

    They framed their answer the way the biologist Theodor Schwann framed cell theory in 1839: as three bold claims. Schwann's first two claims are still in the textbooks. His third turned out to be "horribly wrong". Olah and colleagues offered theirs in the same spirit: stated strongly, so they could be tested.

    Three speculative claims

    Zoom In, 2020

    Claim 1: Features

    Features are the fundamental unit of neural networks. They correspond to directions, and can be rigorously studied and understood.

    Claim 2: Circuits

    Features are connected by weights, forming circuits: small sub-networks that can also be rigorously studied and understood.

    Claim 3: Universality

    Analogous features and circuits form across different models and tasks.

    Our paraphrase of the paper's three claims. Notice the word in Claim 1: directions. The idea from word2vec, that meaning lives in directions, reappears here as a claim about what a network is made of.

    Exhibit one: curve detectors. In an image model called InceptionV1, early in the network, they found neurons that fire for curved edges about 60 pixels across. They come in families, each member tuned to a different angle, together covering all 360 degrees. How do you prove a neuron "detects curves" rather than something that merely correlates with curves? They built the case the way a careful prosecutor would: several independent lines of evidence, each of which could have failed.

    Go deeper: the seven lines of evidence Level 2

    In the paper: Claim 1: Features, "Example 1: Curve Detectors".

    1. Feature visualisation. Generate an image that makes the neuron fire as hard as possible. You get curves.
    2. Dataset examples. The real photos that excite it most contain curves at the expected angle.
    3. Synthetic examples. Drawn test curves of different angles and sizes trigger it as predicted; straight lines and sharp corners don't.
    4. Joint tuning. Rotate an exciting image and the neuron fades while its neighbour in the family takes over.
    5. Feature implementation. Read its incoming weights and you can see a curve-detecting recipe built from earlier curve and line detectors.
    6. Feature use. The neurons downstream that use it are things like circle and spiral detectors.
    7. Handwritten circuits. They set weights by hand, from their understanding, and got a working curve detector that behaves much like the original.

    Their own verdict is measured: these arguments don't rule out "some rare secondary case", but they seem to meet the standards of evidence used in neuroscience. A lawyer would recognise the move. No single exhibit proves the case. Seven that each could have failed, and didn't, come close.

    Exhibit two: a car detector. Later in the network sits a neuron that detects cars (the paper calls it 4c:447). Its weights read like a recipe. A window detector from the layer before excites it at the top of its view and inhibits it at the bottom. A wheel detector does the reverse. A car-body detector excites it, especially at the bottom. Build a car below and see.

    Predict before you press

    Turn the car upside down: wheels on top, body in the middle, windows at the bottom. All the same parts are there. How strongly does the car detector fire?

    Not at all. Wheels in the top row and windows in the bottom row each count against a car. Their pull outweighs the body's push, the total goes below zero, and the neuron's switch (a ReLU, which turns any negative total into zero) stays off. The detector doesn't just check for parts. It checks where they are. Try it with the "Upside down" preset.

    Then the model did something surprising. In the very next layer, instead of keeping one tidy car neuron, it spread the car feature across several neurons whose main job was detecting dogs. The authors called this superposition, and suggested a reason: high-dimensional spaces have only a limited number of fully separate directions, but a vast number of almost separate ones. A model short of neurons can pack more concepts in by sharing. It also leaves neurons that respond to unrelated things. Another InceptionV1 neuron fires for cat faces, cat legs and the fronts of cars. Those are called polysemantic neurons, and they are the villain of Chapters 3 and 4.

    Exhibit three, universality, was the thinnest. Curve detectors and some other early features showed up in several different image models (AlexNet, VGG19, ResNet as well as InceptionV1). The authors called this evidence "anecdotal" and said so plainly. If universality holds, different models are like maps of the same territory drawn by different cartographers at different resolutions, and what you learn reading one transfers to the next. If it fails, every model has to be studied from scratch.

    Go deeper: interpretability as a natural science Level 2

    In the paper: Claim 3: Universality and Interpretability as a Natural Science.

    The authors borrow Thomas Kuhn's idea of a "pre-paradigmatic" field: one without agreement on what the objects of study are, which methods to use, or how to judge results. They say interpretability in 2020 felt like that. Their proposal is to treat a trained network like an organism and make claims about it that could be proven false.

    Circuits suit that well. If you really understand a circuit, you can predict what happens when you edit its weights. And small enough circuits can be checked by plain reasoning, the way you can check a short proof. The price is that each claim covers only a sliver of the network. The hope is that big claims about behaviour can be built up from many small, solid ones.

    That gives us the bet this book follows. In 2021, Olah and colleagues, now at Anthropic, began asking the same questions of transformers, the models that read and write language. They published as they went, in a running series called the Transformer Circuits Thread.

    The end of the prologue

    What we know, and what we don't

    Known: you can know a word by its company

    Describing words by their neighbours gives a map where similar meanings sit close together. Nobody has to say what any word means.

    Known: directions carry meaning, imperfectly

    Relationships like singular to plural, or man to woman, show up as roughly constant arrows. The best 2013 model answered about two-thirds of its analogy questions exactly right.

    Known: maps inherit our biases

    Stereotypes in text become directions in the map. One direction can be measured and removed, though what it correlated with tends to remain.

    Known: loss falls as a power law

    Across many orders of magnitude, loss falls smoothly with parameters (power 0.076), data (0.095) and compute (0.050), as long as nothing else is the bottleneck.

    Known: some neurons are readable

    In image models, curve detectors and a car detector built from windows, body and wheels were checked with several independent lines of evidence.

    Unknown: why scaling works

    The authors had no theory for their laws, and their own formulas predict a breakdown somewhere around 1012 parameters. Where they really stop is still debated.

    Unknown: what appears when

    The laws predict average loss, not abilities. Which skills emerge at which size, and why, the curves don't say.

    Unknown: how much world is inside?

    Board games, maps and timelines have been found inside models trained only on text. How complete and how causal such world models are is an open question.

    Unknown: are language models readable?

    Circuits had been found in image models, and the authors called universality "anecdotal". Whether language models could be read the same way was the open bet.

    Chapter 1

    The Clerks and the Case File

    December 2021. Based on A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (Elhage, Nanda, Olsson et al., Anthropic, 22 December 2021)

    The Prologue ended with a bet: that language models are made of parts we can understand. At the end of 2021 the largest of them, GPT-3, had 96 layers and 175 billion numbers, and nobody could say how it produced a single word.

    The paper behind this chapter framed the job as reverse engineering. Its authors compared it to the work of a programmer handed a program that exists only as machine code, who has to work back to source code a person could read. Their plan was to start with the smallest transformers possible, with zero, one or two layers, and to explain them exactly, with algebra, before going anywhere near a big model.

    To make the small models cleaner, they also removed a whole kind of part, the MLP layers. They called this "a very dramatic simplification", and we'll come back to what it cost them at the end. In exchange they got something rare: models they could take apart completely, on paper, and check against what the models actually did.

    Scene 1

    Words only exist at the doors

    When a transformer reads your sentence, every word gets its own case file. The files are lined up on a long table, in order. At first each file holds very little: which word it is, and where it sits.

    Here's the first surprise. At the front door, a clerk swaps each word for an arrow: a long list of numbers that points somewhere in a space with hundreds or thousands of directions. From then on, nothing inside the building is written in English. Every note in every file is another arrow. Only at the back door does a reader turn the last file back into words, as a guess about what comes next. Words exist only at the entrance and the exit. Everything in between is geometry.

    Front doorword → arrowthe embedding, WE
    The case filesarrows added to arrows, layer after layerthe residual stream
    Back doorarrow → next-word guessthe unembedding, WU

    Between the doors, clerks work through the files, one row of clerks per layer. Each clerk reads a file and adds notes to it. Researchers call this growing file the residual stream. There are two kinds of clerk:

    Messengers look across at earlier files and copy useful notes into the one they're working on. These are the attention heads, and they are the only way information travels between words.

    Thinkers work on one file alone, adding conclusions from what's already in it. These are the MLP layers, the part this paper set aside.

    Notice that a model holds two very different kinds of numbers. The clerks' habits, called the weights, were set during training and are frozen: they're the same for every conversation, like a firm's standing procedures. The case files, called the activations, are opened fresh for each conversation and thrown away afterwards, like the file for today's matter. This paper's boldest move was to read the habits directly, from the weights, without needing to watch any particular case.

    The case files for "Ms Okafor … thanked Ms"

    A sketch

    This is an illustration, not a real model's notes. Real notes aren't words: they're arrows, and we've translated them into English for you. The structure is real, though: one file per word, messengers moving notes between files, thinkers working within a file, and the last file read for the guess. Notice that no step ever rubs a note out.

    Go deeper The case file is a shared ledger

    The paper describes the residual stream as "simply the sum of the output of all the previous layers and the original embedding". It treats it as a communication channel: it "doesn't do any processing itself", and every layer talks to every other layer through it.

    Each clerk reads with a fixed linear projection (it looks at the file through its own fixed lens) and writes by adding an arrow. Nothing is ever overwritten. Think of an append-only ledger, or a blockchain: you can't delete an entry, but you can post a reversing entry that cancels it. The paper saw hints of exactly that: some neurons and heads seem to read a note and write its negative, which the authors suggest may be a kind of "memory management". They flag this as a hint, not a finding.

    The file is big, but not big enough. Small models give it hundreds of directions; large ones tens of thousands. Each messenger reads and writes only a small slice, typically 64 or 128 directions, so different clerks can keep to separate lanes and never interfere. But demand is enormous. A single thinker layer usually has four times as many neurons as the file has directions. The paper's example: halfway up a 50-layer model, the file has about 100 times more neurons before it than it has directions, all trying to talk to 100 times as many neurons after it. Space in the file is precious. That squeeze sets up Chapter 3.

    In the paper: Virtual Weights and the Residual Stream as a Communication Channel, and Subspaces and Residual Stream Bandwidth

    Deepest No desk has a name, and virtual weights

    No privileged basis. The file's directions are like desks in an open-plan office. Because every clerk reads and writes through its own linear lens, you could rotate the whole office, turning every lens to match, and the model would behave identically. So there's no reason for "desk 17" on its own to mean anything. The paper says the residual stream has no privileged basis. Meaning lives in directions that may cut across many desks, which is why later chapters hunt for directions rather than reading desks one by one.

    Virtual weights. Because the channel is linear, you can multiply a writer's output weights by a reader's input weights, WI2WO1, and get a direct line between two layers, even with many layers in between. It tells you how much a later clerk reads what an earlier one wrote. Those lines don't exist anywhere in the model as separate numbers. They appear only when you multiply things out, which is the trick the rest of this chapter keeps using.

    Can you read a middle file as words? Only roughly. The paper cites a trick called the logit lens: hold the back-door reader up to a middle file and see what guess is forming. It works to a degree because the file is a running sum. The paper's authors went the other way and multiplied out the weights instead, deciding that the residual stream "isn't actually the fundamental object to study".

    Scene 2

    Zero clerks: the one-word guesser

    Start with the simplest case: no clerks at all. The front door turns the word into an arrow, and the back door immediately turns that arrow into a guess. Nothing earlier in the sentence can reach the last file. So the most the model can learn is "after this word, that word usually comes next", which is called a bigram table.

    The paper writes the whole zero-layer model in one line:

    T = WU WE

    WE
    the front door: one arrow per word.
    WU
    the back door: turns an arrow into a score for every possible next word.
    T
    their product: a table with one row per current word and one column per next word.

      The paper makes two further points about this little product. First, it never goes away. Every transformer, however deep, contains a WUWE term, the direct path, where a word's arrow flows straight from the front door to the back door. In bigger models, other parts handle most grammar, so the direct path ends up holding the leftovers: bigrams no general rule explains. The paper's example is that "Barack" is often followed by "Obama".

      Second, the back door is not the front door run backwards. People call WU the "unembedding", but the paper notes we shouldn't expect it to be the inverse of WE. The arrow for "ms" coming in has to predict "reyes" going out.

      Deepest The same squeeze as the Prologue's word map

      The paper points to a 2014 observation by Levy and Goldberg: many early word embeddings can be seen as squeezing a big table of word statistics into a few directions. The Prologue's word map did exactly that, squeezing co-occurrence counts down to two directions. A zero-layer transformer does it too. T has 59 × 59 = 3,481 entries here, but if the file has only d desks, T is built from 59 × d numbers at each door. That makes it a low-rank table: it can only express patterns that fit through d directions. When you set one desk and watch the score stall, you're watching that limit bite.

      Scene 3

      One row of messengers: where to look, and what to copy

      Give the model one row of messengers. Now, while working on the last file, a messenger can look back at every earlier file and decide which ones are worth copying from.

      The paper's central observation is that each messenger does two separate jobs, and that they can be studied one at a time. In its words, which words to move information from "is completely separable from" what information is moved and how it is written.

      Where to look. The current file poses a question, such as "is there a name earlier on?", and every earlier file carries a label. Where question and label match, the messenger pays attention. This is the QK circuit, for query and key.

      What to copy. Having looked, the messenger writes something into the current file, which changes the odds of each next word. This is the OV circuit, for output and value.

      Put the two together and every messenger becomes a pile of rules with three slots: an earlier word A, the current word B, and a next word C that becomes more likely. The paper calls these skip-trigrams and writes them "A … B → C". Here are some it found in real one-layer models. Try to guess each next word before you tap.

      Most of these heads spend much of their effort on one humble trick: copying. If a word appears earlier, raise the odds that it appears again, but only at places where it could plausibly fit. Seeing "Reyes" earlier makes "Reyes" likelier after a later "Ms". The paper calls this a "very primitive" kind of in-context learning, and it will matter again in Chapter 2.

      Go deeper The path expansion, in one equation

      Because every clerk adds to the file, you can expand the whole model into a sum of routes from the front door to the back door. For a one-layer model the paper gets:

      T = Id ⊗ WUWE + Σh Ah ⊗ (WUWOVhWE)

      Ah = softmax( tT · WETWQKhWE · t )

      In plain words: the guess equals the one-word table from Scene 2, plus one contribution from each messenger h. Each contribution is "where this messenger looked" (Ah) combined with "what the word it looked at says about the next word" (WUWOVWE). The "Id ⊗" on the first route just means it stays in its own file.

      The two jobs become two giant tables, each with one row and one column per word in the vocabulary. The QK circuit, WETWQKWE, scores how much each current word wants to look at each earlier word. The OV circuit, WUWOVWE, says how each looked-at word shifts the odds of each next word. With about 50,000 tokens, one OV table has around 2.5 billion entries.

      "we revealed the one-layer attention-only model to be a compressed Chinese room, and we're left with a giant pile of cards."Elhage et al., 2021, on what the expanded tables amount to

      The cards are huge but secretly simple: each table has rank of only 64 or 128, the width of the messenger's slice of the file. Keys, queries and values, the terms you'll see in most explanations of transformers, turn out to be "intermediary by-products" of computing these two tables.

      In the paper: The Path Expansion Trick, Splitting Attention Head terms into Query-Key and Output-Value Circuits, Interpretation as Skip-Trigrams

      Deepest How to spot a copying head from its weights alone

      Copying means a word raises its own odds. In matrix language, that's an OV table with positive eigenvalues: some group of words that, when looked at, boosts that same group. A random matrix has roughly as many negative eigenvalues as positive ones, and many complex ones. So a head whose eigenvalues lean heavily positive is very unlikely to be an accident.

      Using this test on a one-layer model with 12 heads, the authors found that "10 out of 12 heads are significantly copying", which matched what they saw by reading the tables. They're careful about it: positive eigenvalues are "quite strong evidence" of copying, but they "shouldn't be considered a dispositive proof", because a matrix can have all-positive eigenvalues and still lower some words' own odds.

      In the paper: Copying / Primitive In-Context Learning, Detecting Copying Behavior

      Scene 4

      The bug that proves the theory

      If "where to look" and "what to copy" really are separate, a messenger should make a very particular mistake. Take the idioms "keep … in mind" and "keep … at bay". Suppose one messenger handles both. From "in" and from "at", it looks back at "keep". Then it copies what "keep" suggests: "mind" and "bay". But the copying step never sees whether it started from "in" or "at". So the messenger must also push "keep … in bay" and "keep … at mind".

      It's like a firm's form letter built from two separate lists: one says which earlier document to cite, the other says what remedy that document supports. The second list never sees who the letter is for, so every recipient who triggers that citation gets every remedy.

      The paper found exactly these mistakes in real heads, and called them skip-trigram "bugs". Try to fix one yourself.

      The puzzle

      Set the switches so "keep it in" predicts mind and "keep them at" predicts bay, with no cross-talk. Can you do it with one messenger?

      Fix the "keep … in bay" bug

      Real calculation · hand-set switches

      The maths here is a real one-layer attention calculation: each switch sets a score in the messenger's QK or OV table, attention is a softmax over the earlier words, and the odds are a softmax over 22 possible next words (mind, bay and 20 others). When a messenger finds nothing it wants, it rests on the start of the text, a habit the paper observed in real heads, and so writes almost nothing.

      Go deeper Why the bug was good news

      The paper describes the problem as a "factored form". A messenger can't store an arbitrary rule f(A, B, C) about three words. It can only store something shaped like f1(A, B) × f2(A, C): one part for where to look, one part for what to copy, with A as the only link between them. Any rule that needs B and C to depend on each other directly is out of reach for a single head.

      A second messenger fixes it, as you may have found. But messengers are expensive: each has its own attention pattern and its own 64 or 128 directions. The authors suspect that tolerating the bug is "a good trade for the model on balance". They report seeing such bugs frequently in the expanded weights, and they were pleased, calling it "an early demonstration of using interpretability to understand model failures". A theory that predicts the model's specific mistakes is much more convincing than one that only explains its successes.

      They left open how much these bugs cost the model, and whether they survive in larger models, where other parts might paper over them.

      In the paper: Skip-Trigram "Bugs"

      Scene 5

      Every clerk casts a vote

      Because clerks only ever add notes, the final guess is the sum of every clerk's contribution. Each route through the model awards points to each possible next word, and the points become odds. So you can do something lawyers will recognise: produce an itemised account of who contributed what, switch one contributor off, and see what changes.

      That matters well beyond tiny models. If a decision is a sum of contributions, you can ask which part caused it, the way a court apportions liability or an auditor traces each entry in a ledger back to the person who posted it.

      Go deeper Freezing the attention, and when receipts mislead

      The freezing trick. Is it fair to study the two jobs separately, when "where to look" passes through a softmax that isn't linear? The paper's answer is a thought experiment. Run the model once and record where every messenger looked. Run it again with those attention patterns frozen. Now the output is a plain linear function of the input words, and every route's contribution can be read off exactly. That's what our receipt does.

      Attention isn't the whole story. A long-running debate asks whether attention patterns explain a model's decisions. The paper's view is that a messenger acting alone is "straightforwardly interpretable", but messengers in different layers can interact, and then attention patterns can mislead. Its example comes in the next scene: an auditor who looked only at the attention of an induction head would credit the wrong clerk.

      The limit of the receipt. This exact split works because this model has no thinkers. Thinkers pass the file through a non-linear step, so their contributions no longer add up so neatly. Chapter 6 shows how researchers later got itemised accounts out of large models anyway.

      Scene 6

      Two layers: clerks who read each other's notes

      With two layers, a second-row messenger can read notes that a first-row messenger left behind. The paper calls this composition, and it's where small models stop being lookup tables. In the authors' words, two-layer models become "something much more like a computer program running an algorithm".

      Here's how it fixes the mix-up above. A first-row messenger works on every file and writes one fact: "the word before me was…". This is a previous-token head. Now the file for "novak" carries the note "the word before me was ms". A second-row messenger working on the final "ms" asks: which earlier file has the label "the word before me was ms"? That's the file for "novak", so it copies "novak".

      That two-clerk team is an induction head. Where a one-layer copier follows the pattern [B] … [A] → [B], an induction head follows [A][B] … [A] → [B]: it remembers what came after A last time. The paper found these in its two-layer models and tested them in the hardest way it could, on repeated sequences of completely random tokens, where no grammar or statistics can help. Chapter 2 is the story of how much they turned out to matter.

      There are exactly three ways a later messenger can use an earlier one's notes, one for each thing it reads: its question (Q), the labels it matches against (K), or the notes it copies (V). Can you tell them apart?

      Go deeper What they actually found inside a two-layer model

      The authors measured how strongly each second-layer head reads from each first-layer head, for all three kinds of composition. In the model they studied, most heads weren't composing at all; roughly, they were just more skip-trigram tables. The striking signal was K-composition with one first-layer head that mostly attends to the previous token. The heads reading from it were induction heads.

      They then checked the theory from the weights. An induction head needs a copying OV table and a "same-word matching" QK table. Plotted by how positive those two sets of eigenvalues are, "all the induction heads turn out to be in an extreme right hand corner". The authors point out this wasn't circular: nothing about strong composition required it to be positive.

      This is also where looking only at attention would mislead. An induction head's attention points straight at "novak", so you might credit "novak"'s file alone. But the decision of where to look depends entirely on the previous-token head, one layer down, that wrote the label. Miss the K-composition and you misunderstand both clerks.

      In the paper: Three Kinds of Composition, Induction Heads, Checking the Mechanistic Theory

      Deepest Virtual heads, and a correction the authors published

      Virtual attention heads. V-composition is different in kind. When one messenger copies a note that another messenger had already copied, the pair acts like a single long-distance messenger, with its own attention pattern (Ah2Ah1) and its own copy table (WOVh2WOVh1). The number of these grows with the square of the number of heads, so in principle there are a lot of them. But in the small two-layer model, an ablation test found their combined effect was small. The authors speculate they may matter much more in big models; that remains open.

      A published correction. After the paper came out, the authors found a bug in a linear-algebra library they had written to handle these huge low-rank tables. It affected one diagram, the composition measurements. The corrected version shows more composition than first reported: two of the induction heads also read from a head that looks at the last several words, not just the previous one. The central finding, K-composition with the previous-token head, held up. The paper carries the correction openly at the end.

      Scene 7

      What does "understand" mean?

      So had they fully understood a one-layer model? The authors gave a careful answer. Every number is now in context: there is "no longer any algorithmic mystery". But the understanding is the kind you have of a giant regression, or of a large database you know how to query. The tables are far too big to hold in your head, and summarising them well was still an open problem.

      And then there were the thinkers they'd set aside. MLP layers hold about two thirds of a standard transformer's parameters, and the authors were blunt that their absence was "a major weakness of our work". They'd tried. Apart from some neurons early in the model that respond to clusters of short phrases, they found the neurons much harder to make sense of than the attention heads.

      Over a year later, in a note added to the paper, they named their suspect. The thinkers seemed hard to read because of something called superposition: too many ideas sharing too few neurons. That is Chapter 3.

      Chapter 2

      The Copycat Trick

      Based on In-context Learning and Induction Heads (Olsson, Elhage, Nanda et al., Anthropic, 2022)

      It started with a bump. Training curves are supposed to be dull: the model's error falls quickly at first, then more and more slowly, like a ball rolling to rest. But while training a small two-layer model, Chris Olah noticed a little kink early on, where the curve briefly sagged faster than it should. In the paper that followed, it turned out to be the only place in training where the curve broke its usual shape.

      Catherine Olsson led the investigation that came next. The team trained and dissected 34 models, from one layer up to 40 layers and 13 billion parameters, saving snapshots throughout training and knocking out attention heads more than 50,000 times. They were chasing one question: is Chapter 1's two-clerk copycat team the engine behind a model's ability to learn from the text in front of it?

      This chapter follows their case, step by step. Each scene starts simply. When you want the real mechanism and the numbers, open the Go deeper panels.

      Scene 1

      Learning without changing

      Here's the puzzle. Once a model has finished training, its billions of numbers, its weights, are frozen. Nothing inside it changes while you talk to it. And yet it plainly learns from what you tell it.

      Lawyers rely on the same skill every day. Page one of a contract says "Northwind Logistics Ltd (the 'Supplier')", and from then on every careful reader knows exactly who "the Supplier" is. Nobody rewired their brain. They read the definition, and they carry it forward. Introduce a made-up name, a new term or an odd format to a language model, and a few lines later it's using it correctly too.

      Researchers call this in-context learning: learning from the text in the model's window (its context), rather than from training. The 2022 paper asked which clerks make it possible.

      So what is AI "memory"?

      When a chat product "remembers" you between conversations, it usually works like a learning log that a new session reads before it starts. The product saves notes somewhere outside the model, then slips them into the text of your next conversation. The weights haven't changed. The model is simply reading a longer file. Everything in this chapter happens inside one context window, while the weights stay exactly as they are.

      Go deeperTwo ways to measure it, and an argument about the word "learning"

      The paper notes two common ways to think about in-context learning. One is few-shot learning, made famous by GPT-3: show the model a few worked examples of a task (say, English → French) and see whether it does the next one. The authors call this the "micro" view, because it depends on which tasks you pick.

      The other, which they adopt, is the "macro" view from Kaplan and colleagues: simply measure how much better the model predicts words late in a document than early on. It doesn't need anyone to choose tasks. Its weakness is the flip side: it lumps every kind of context use together. You'll compute this measure yourself in Scene 4.

      There's a subtle argument hidden in a footnote. Some people call this ability meta-learning, which implies the model learns a genuinely new skill. Others say it is "locating" a skill it already has, working out which job it's being asked to do. The authors call the question both controversial and not entirely precise. The contract example leans towards "locating": you already knew what a supplier is, and the definition only tells you which party it is.

      The authors also flag why this matters for safety: in-context learning means a model's behaviour can, in their words, in some sense "change" during inference, without further training, which makes long conversations harder to predict.

      Scene 2

      The copycat rule

      An induction head follows one simple rule:

      If you've seen A followed by B before, then the next time you see A, guess B.

      The paper writes it as [A][B] … [A] → [B]. It's the trick you need for "the Supplier", for names, for repeated phrases, and for any pattern that appears earlier in the same text. After "Northwind Logistics Ltd" has appeared once, the next "Northwind" makes "Logistics" a very good bet.

      The rule doesn't care what A and B are. That's what makes it powerful: it isn't a memorised table of which word follows which. It works on names the model has never seen in training, as long as they appeared earlier in this document.

      To prove a head really follows the rule, the researchers used a deliberately meaningless test. Take 25 random tokens and repeat them four times.       Go deeperHow the paper defines an induction head

      In 2021 the term was a bit loose: a cluster of behaviours that tended to appear together. For this paper the authors pinned it down by behaviour alone, because the weights of big models with MLPs are too tangled to inspect. On a repeated sequence of random tokens, an induction head must show both:

      Prefix matching (a "where to look" property, the QK circuit): it attends to earlier tokens whose preceding token matches the current one. The score above is our version of their evaluator: 25 random tokens, repeated 4 times, after a start token, and we measure the share of attention that lands on those positions.

      Copying (a "what to copy" property, the OV circuit): its output raises the score of the very token it attends to. The paper's copying score runs from −1 to 1.

      Why random tokens? Because on real text a head could look like it's predicting well by guessing a plausible next word. On random tokens there's nothing plausible to guess. Only genuine copying from the earlier repeat helps.

      A consequence: induction heads repeat whole sequences. Given "Ms Reyes asked the witness about the contract … Ms Reyes asked", they push the model towards "the witness about the contract". Repeating an arbitrary sequence after one example is already a simple form of few-shot learning.

      Scene 3

      Two clerks, one trick

      Remember the mix-up at the end of Chapter 1? In "mr osei and ms novak spoke then the judge thanked ms", a single messenger found both names but couldn't tell which one belonged to "ms".

      An induction head is a team of two clerks:

      Clerk 1, in an early row, works on every file and writes down one fact: "the word just before me was…". So the file for "novak" gets the note "before me: ms". That's the previous-word head from the grid above.

      Clerk 2, in a later row, works on the last file, "ms". It asks: "which earlier file has the note before me: ms?" That's the file for "novak", so it copies "novak" forward.

      Clerk 2 can only ask that question because Clerk 1 left the note. This is the composition from Chapter 1: one clerk's notes changing where another clerk looks.

The paper saw the same thing in real models: after two "Mrs Dursley"s in the first paragraph of Harry Potter, the first "Mrs Potter" became harder to predict once induction heads had formed.

      Go deeperWhy it takes two layers

      When a messenger decides where to look, it compares two things only: the file it's working on (the query) and the label on the file it's considering (the key). Nothing else. So a lone messenger can ask "is this file about ms?", but never "was the word before this file ms?", because that fact isn't on the label.

      Clerk 1 fixes this by writing the previous word into every file. Clerk 2's key is then built partly from Clerk 1's note. The paper calls this K-composition: a later head's keys read from what an earlier head wrote. There's a neat split of duties. When deciding where to look, the induction head practically ignores the attended word itself and only checks the note about its predecessor. When deciding what to copy, the attended word is all that matters.

      That's why one-layer models can't build induction heads, and, as Scene 5 shows, why they never get the big jump in in-context learning.

      Deeper stillIndependent replications, and a second recipe

      The paper includes comments from outside researchers who replicated the finding. A team at Redwood Research found induction heads reliably in two-layer attention-only models, with a previous-token head in the first layer. When they swapped the previous-token head's attention for a perfect "look one back" pattern, the model kept 99% of that head's contribution to loss. A simple idealised version of the induction head's attention kept about 65%, and adding longer matches like [A][B][C] … [A][B] → [C] recovered roughly another 10%.

      Not every model uses Clerk 1's note. In GPT-2 the authors saw signs of a different recipe, "pointer arithmetic": an earlier head copies the position of the previous A, and the induction head shifts that position one step forward. The paper's own models couldn't use this trick, because of how they encode positions.

      Scene 4

      Measuring the learning

      To link induction heads to in-context learning, the researchers needed a number they could track through training. They chose something blunt and simple. Take thousands of documents. Measure how badly the model guesses the 50th token, and how badly it guesses the 500th. The 500th has 450 more tokens of context behind it, so if the model learns from context, it should do better there.

      ICL score = L(500) − L(50)

      In words: the loss (how surprised the model was by the right answer) at token 500, minus the loss at token 50, averaged over documents. A more negative score means more learning from context. Loss is measured in nats: if the model gave the right word probability p, its loss is −ln p. A drop of 0.4 nats means the model gives the right word about 1.5 times more probability, on (geometric) average.

      The paper's headline numbers: before the phase change, models showed less than 0.15 nats of in-context learning. After it, about 0.4 nats, and then it stayed there for the rest of training.       Go deeperPulling the average apart, and a puzzle about big models

      A loss curve is an average over millions of individual predictions. The researchers took it apart token by token, which they call per-token loss analysis. Comparing the start and end of the phase change on the first paragraph of Harry Potter, they found that most of the change came from tokens that repeat: the model got much better at the fourth token of "The Dursleys" (which appears several times), and worse at the first "Mrs Potter", because both earlier "Mrs" had been followed by "Dursley". The authors are clear this is one passage, an illustration rather than systematic evidence. The word strip in the toy shows the same pattern.

      Why tokens 50 and 500? The authors chose them "somewhat arbitrarily": 500 is near the end of a 512-token window, and by 50 the basics (language, kind of document) are established. They checked that other choices don't change the conclusions.

      Scene 5

      The bump in the curve

      Now back to the bump. When the team lined up their measurements against training time, four things happened in the same narrow window, in model after model:

      1. The in-context learning score jumped. 2. Induction heads formed. 3. The loss curve showed its bump, a stretch of visibly steeper improvement. 4. The model's overall behaviour changed direction (in a summary of its per-token losses).

      In the paper's main figures the window falls at roughly 2.5 to 5 billion tokens of training. For the largest models that's maybe 1–2% of the way through. The authors call it a phase change, an abrupt shift with both a visible, big-picture side (the curves) and a microscopic side (the heads), perhaps analogous to e.g. ice melting.

      One-layer models, which can't build the two-clerk team, showed no bump, no induction heads and little in-context learning. Then came the cleverest experiment in the paper. If induction heads really drive the jump, then making them easier to build should move the jump. So they gave one-layer models a shortcut, called smeared keys, that lets a single clerk see a little of the previous word.

      Go deeperRuling out other suspects, and the smeared-key formula

      A good investigator checks the alibis. Could the bump be caused by the training schedule? The authors checked: the window doesn't line up with a scheduled change in learning rate, warm-up or weight decay. (The small models' warm-up ends at 1.5 billion tokens, and a weight-decay change at about 5 billion causes a separate small hiccup.) Small models trained on a different dataset showed the same phase change. One caveat they state themselves: apart from that test, all the models saw the same tokens in the same order.

      Over 75% of the final in-context learning forms inside the window. It's tempting to conclude that induction heads cause at least that much. The authors resist: they have only shown that the two arrive together.

      Here is the smeared-key change, in the paper's notation:

      kj ← σ(α) · kj + (1 − σ(α)) · kj−1

      In words: each file's label k becomes a blend of its own label and the label of the file just before it. The blend is set by one trainable dial α per head (σ squashes it to between 0 and 1). With that blend, a single clerk can do Clerk 1's job itself, so even one layer can build an induction head.

      The authors don't oversell it. This shows induction heads are the minimal mechanism for the big jump. Larger models might have more going on, and the mechanism could change later in training.

      Scene 6

      More than copying

      Copying exact words is useful, but it can't explain translation, or picking up a rule from examples. So the authors went looking inside a 40-layer, 13-billion-parameter model for induction heads that do something looser:

      If you've seen something like A followed by something like B, then after A, guess something like B.

      The paper writes it [A*][B*] … [A] → [B] and calls it "fuzzy" or "nearest neighbour" pattern completion. They found heads that pass the strict random-token test and do much more. One translates, attending to the word in an earlier English, French or German sentence that should come next. Another does the puzzle below.

      Each of these heads counts as an induction head by the strict definition, scoring well on copying and prefix matching on random tokens. The very same head also does the fancier job. As the authors put it, The comparison is not a metaphor or a blurring of the definition.

Or the head copies literally when it reads raw words, and abstractly when earlier layers have already placed, say, "cat" and "chat" near each other. Mechanistically, they argue, the same machinery works as an in-context nearest neighbour method: the earlier text is a set of examples, and more examples help.

      Go deeperThe defence's best arguments

      The authors list the weaknesses of their own case, which is part of why it's persuasive.

      A hidden common cause. Maybe the phase change is really the moment a model learns to compose layers at all. That would produce induction heads and other composition tricks at once. Bigger models have more heads to spare for such tricks, so above some size they might matter more than induction heads.

      Few snapshots. The large models were saved only 14 or 15 times during training, so "at the same moment" is a coarser claim for them.

      A flat score can hide change. The ICL score stays at 0.4 nats, but the mechanisms producing it might keep changing. The case is stronger for what happens at the phase change than at the end of training.

      MLPs complicate knockouts. In attention-only models, the final guess is a sum of contributions from each head plus a direct path, and the direct path can't use context, so knockouts measure contributions cleanly. Once thinker layers are added, removing a head can disturb them in roundabout ways.

      The paper also explains why it reports evidence this indirect at all: it is likely to be common as interpretability advances, and the authors wanted to establish a norm of reporting it even when it is not fully conclusive.

      Deeper stillWhy this paper mattered beyond induction heads

      As far as the authors knew, this was the first time anyone had explained a phase change in machine learning by pointing to a specific mechanism. They describe it as a possible "Rosetta stone" between two worlds: the smooth statistics of scaling laws (the Prologue's world) and the circuit-by-circuit view of interpretability.

      It also bears on safety. Capabilities that appear abruptly are hard to prepare for. And some had worried that in-context learning might secretly be a "mesa-optimizer", a model running its own hidden optimisation process. In their small models, the authors write, the main mechanism appears to be induction heads, and they saw no evidence of mesa-optimizers.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: models learn from context

      With weights frozen, models predict later words better than early ones. After the phase change, models of every size gained about 0.4 nats between token 50 and token 500.

      Known: induction heads copy patterns

      A two-clerk team carries out [A][B] … [A] → [B], using one clerk's note to steer the other's attention. One layer isn't enough.

      Known: they arrive together, suddenly

      Induction heads, the jump in in-context learning and the bump in the loss curve share one early window in training, and moving the heads (smeared keys) moved the jump.

      Known, for small models: they do most of it

      In small attention-only models, knocking out induction heads removes almost all in-context learning. The authors grade this strong, causal evidence.

      Unknown: is this most of it in big models?

      For large models the case is circumstantial: correlation, striking examples and continuity with small models. Other composition circuits might do much of the work.

      Unknown: why exactly 0.4 nats?

      Why tiny and huge models end up with the same score was, in the authors' word, a mystery.

      Unknown: learning that sticks

      In-context learning vanishes when the conversation ends. Models whose weights keep updating as they work ("continual learning") are an open question for later chapters, not something this paper studied.

      Unknown: what about the thinkers?

      The cleanest results came from models without MLP "thinker" layers. In real models the thinkers are everywhere, and their neurons are hard to read.

      Chapter 3

      The Crowded Office

      Based on Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Schiefer et al., Anthropic and Harvard, September 2022)

      By 2022, interpretability researchers had a nagging puzzle. In some models, a single neuron really did mean one thing. Vision models had famous "curve detector" neurons that fired for curves and nothing else. But in language models, neurons like that were rare. Most of them fired for a strange mix of unrelated things.

      The authors of this chapter's paper put the puzzle in one line:

      "Why is it that neurons sometimes align with features and sometimes don't?"Elhage et al., 2022, opening section

      Their answer has a name, superposition, and a method: build the smallest models anyone could imagine, small enough that you can see every number inside them, and watch what they do. What they found was stranger and more beautiful than they expected: sudden phase changes like water freezing, and concepts arranging themselves into triangles, pentagons and shapes from 3D geometry, all without anyone asking.

      You'll rerun most of those experiments yourself in this chapter. Each scene starts simply. When you want more, open the Go deeper drawers: they hold the paper's real mechanism, its own terms and numbers, and a pointer to the section, so you can read the original alongside.

      First, a reminder of the characters. Inside a language model, every idea it can think about is stored as an arrow: a direction in a vast space. Researchers call it latent space, "hidden space", because nobody labelled any of its directions.

      The case file

      What the model is thinking about right now. It's passed from clerk to clerk as the model reads your words.

      The clerks

      The model's layers. Each one reads the file, adds a few notes, and passes it on.

      The concepts

      Ideas like "French", "a legal term" or "octopus". The paper calls them features. There are millions of them.

      The desks

      The directions available to store concepts in: the paper's dimensions. There are only a few thousand.

      Scene 1

      The neuron that couldn't make up its mind

      The hopeful plan went like this. A model is made of tiny parts called neurons. If each neuron stood for one idea, you could read a model like a dictionary: here's the "French" neuron, here's the "angry" neuron.

      Sometimes it worked. But again and again, researchers found neurons like this one, which lights up for things that have nothing to do with each other.

      Meet one jumbled neuron

      Illustration of a real finding

      The pattern is real; the example texts and bar heights are ours. A 2023 Anthropic study (Chapter 4) found a single neuron in a small language model that responded to academic citations, English dialogue, web requests and Korean text. Researchers call neurons like this polysemantic, "many meanings". The 2022 paper set out to explain why they exist.

      Go deeperTwo forces pulling in opposite directions

      The paper starts from a working assumption it calls the linear representation hypothesis, which has two parts. Decomposability: what a model is thinking can be split into separate concepts, each understandable on its own. Linearity: each concept is a direction, and "how much" of it is present is how far the file points that way.

      Then it names two forces. Some layers have a privileged basis: something in the design (an activation function applied to each neuron separately) makes the neuron directions special, which nudges concepts to line up with single neurons. Pulling the other way is superposition: storing more concepts than there are directions, which pushes concepts off the neurons and smears each one across many of them.

      What counts as a "feature"? The authors admit it's hard to define. They consider three candidates and lean towards the third: a feature is a property of the input that a large enough model would reliably give its own neuron. In smaller models, the same feature may have to share.

      A real example from the paper's discussion: the first thinker layer of a language model may need to tell apart the English word "die" from the German article "die" (and the Dutch and Afrikaans words spelled the same way). Each of those meanings is rare, and rare is exactly what the theory says gets squeezed. The authors report that, in their experience, neurons in that first layer are "extremely polysemantic".

      Go deeperThe paper's three assumptions about concepts

      To build a toy world, the authors had to decide what concepts are like. They made three assumptions:

      1. Concepts are sparse. Most of the time, a given concept is absent. In their words, "most tokens don't refer to Martin Luther King". In the toy, each concept is switched off with probability S (the sparsity), and otherwise takes a random strength between 0 and 1. So 1 − S is how often it shows up.

      2. There are more concepts than neurons. A language model could usefully know about every person ever written about. The authors note, with some amusement, that language models know about people of quite modest fame, "such as several of the authors of this paper", who presumably turn up far less than once in a million tokens.

      3. Concepts differ in importance. Some help the model's score far more than others. In the toy, each concept gets an importance I, a weight on how much its mistakes count.

      So every concept has two numbers: how often it appears, and how much it matters. Hold on to those two numbers. The rest of the chapter is about what happens as they change.

      Scene 3

      A room that's much bigger than it looks

      For a model, a "desk" is a direction. Two concepts stay completely out of each other's way when their arrows are at a right angle (perpendicular, or orthogonal). On a flat page you can only fit two arrows at right angles: one across, one up. In a space with 1,000 directions, you can fit exactly 1,000.

      But here is the strange part. If you're willing to accept almost perpendicular, the count explodes. To see why, think about how you measure the overlap between two arrows: you multiply them coordinate by coordinate and add up the results. For two random arrows in 1,000 directions, that's 1,000 tiny numbers, each equally likely to be positive or negative. Like 1,000 coin flips, they mostly cancel out. The leftover is typically about 1 divided by the square root of 1,000, roughly 0.03, which corresponds to an angle of about 88°.

      Go deeperRoom isn't the same as free room

      Almost perpendicular still means slightly overlapping. Whenever one concept is active, every concept that shares its space receives a faint false signal. The paper calls this interference. Pack in more arrows and each one gets noisier. So geometry alone doesn't settle how many concepts a model can usefully hold: it also depends on how often concepts are active at the same time.

      The paper's related work section connects this to compressed sensing, a branch of mathematics about recovering a signal from fewer measurements than it has entries, which is possible when the signal is sparse. Borrowing a lower bound from that field, the authors estimate the number of concepts their toy can store and conclude that it "is linear in m but modulated by the sparsity": it grows in proportion to the number of directions m, multiplied by a factor that grows as concepts get rarer. That's a more sober figure than the exponential count above, and the toy model is a far weaker decoder than the algorithms compressed sensing uses.

      Here's what you should see, and it's what the researchers saw.

      When concepts are common, the model behaves like a cautious filing clerk. It keeps only the two most important concepts, gives each a private direction at a right angle, and simply gives up on the other three. Sharing would cause too many clashes.

      As concepts get rarer, it starts squeezing more in. Often the first step is three: concept 1 keeps a private desk while concepts 2 and 3 share the other one, pointing in opposite directions like the two ends of a see-saw. Then four, as two see-saws. Finally all five, spread into a pentagon, each with two-fifths of a desk. Nobody told it to make a pentagon.

      That squeezing is superposition: more concepts than directions, sharing the space because they seldom appear at the same time. The paper's headline: a model can "represent more features than it has dimensions".

      Predict before you press

      Untick "Filter on the way out" and slide to "very rarely". With the filter gone, the model is purely linear: it just multiplies and adds. How many concepts will it store?

      Two, at every setting. Without the filter, there's no way to throw away the faint false signals, so sharing always costs more than it gains. The best a linear model can do is keep the two most important concepts at right angles, the same answer as a classic statistics technique called principal component analysis. The paper's surprise is that one small filter, a ReLU, changes the answer completely.

      Who gets a desk?

      Asked to guess which ideas a crowded model would protect, the owner of this book said: the most connected, the ones with the most impact. The maths agrees. What a model loses by dropping a concept is its importance multiplied by how often it shows up. A concept that matters a lot but almost never appears is cheap to drop, and so is one that appears constantly but barely matters. Set importance to "steep" and watch concept 5 struggle to get in at all.

      Go deeperThe model, in the paper's notation

      The paper writes the model in one line. Here it is once, then in words.

      h = Wx     x′ = ReLU(Wᵀh + b) = ReLU(WᵀWx + b)

      x is the list of 5 concept strengths. W is a table that turns them into 2 numbers, h: the case file. Each concept's column of W is its arrow, which is exactly what the toy draws. To rebuild, the model reads the file back along each concept's arrow (that's Wᵀh), adds a small per-concept offset b (the bias), and applies ReLU, a filter that turns any negative number into zero.

      loss = Σi Ii (xi − x′i)²

      The score to minimise: the squared error for each concept, weighted by its importance Ii. Our toy uses importance 0.8i by default; the paper's first experiment used 0.7i with 20 concepts and 5 directions, and found the same story.

      The paper explains the result as a tug of war between two forces. Feature benefit: storing another concept lowers the error on it. Interference: storing it at an angle to others adds false signals to them. In a linear model, the interference always wins once you run out of perpendicular directions. With the ReLU, a negative false signal is simply cut to zero, so it costs nothing when only one concept is active. That's why the shapes it finds favour opposite-pointing arrows.

      In the paper: Demonstrating Superposition (Experiment Setup, Basic Results, Mathematical Understanding).

      Deeper stillMeasuring "a share of a desk"

      The bars in the toy use the paper's feature dimensionality:

      Di = ‖Wi‖² / Σj (Ŵi · Wj)²

      In words: how strongly concept i is stored, divided by how much everyone (itself included) shows up along its arrow. A concept alone on its direction scores 1. Each end of a see-saw scores 1/(1+1) = 1/2. A concept that isn't stored scores 0. Check the pentagon: each gets 2/5.

      Notice the total. When the model packs efficiently, the shares add up to the number of directions, here 2: 1+1, or 1+½+½, or ½×4, or ⅖×5. The paper observed the same: "the dimensionality of all features add up to the number of embedding dimensions when the features are 'packed efficiently'".

      The paper also tracks a whole-model version, the "dimensions per feature". In a larger run (400 concepts, 30 directions, all equally important) it found the number doesn't slide smoothly as concepts get rarer. It sticks at 1, then sticks at ½ (see-saws everywhere) over a wide range, before moving on. The authors note it "very vaguely resembles the fractional quantum Hall effect" in physics.

      And the error line under the toy? The paper saw that training doesn't always improve smoothly. Concepts jump between arrangements, and the error drops in a sudden step each time, like an electron hopping between energy levels. Start from scratch at "one time in twenty" and watch for steps. Chapter 2's induction heads appeared in a sudden jump too.

      In the paper: Uniform Superposition, Feature Dimensionality, Learning Dynamics: Discrete "Energy Level" Jumps.

      Scene 5

      Ice, water, steam: the phase diagram

      The paper noticed that each concept seems to meet one of three fates. It's left out. It shares a desk (superposition). Or it gets a desk of its own. And the switch between fates looked sudden, the way water doesn't become slightly icy: it freezes.

      To isolate this, they shrank the problem as far as it goes: 2 concepts, 1 desk. Concept A has importance 1. You choose how important concept B, the newcomer, is, and how often both show up.       Go deeperWhy "phase change" is the right word

      In the paper's two-concept model the best arrangement doesn't drift from one strategy to another. It jumps. As you cross a boundary, the arrows' lengths and angles change abruptly. The authors confirmed this in their theoretical version: the error curves of two strategies cross, so the best achievable error has a kink, a "first-order phase change" in physics language. (They use "phase change" loosely, meaning a sudden change, without claiming the stricter physics sense.)

      They repeated it with 3 concepts in 2 directions and found more regions, including one where the newcomer gets a desk and the other two share a see-saw. Then they warned that the real picture is richer still, as the next scene shows.

      Their analogy: we say water has three phases, but ice alone has many crystal forms. "Superposition" likewise isn't one thing. It has many phases of its own.

      Why does this matter beyond toys? If superposition switches on and off in sharp phases, there may be a regime where a model has none at all. The authors call this "Phase Changes as Cause For Hope": push a model across the boundary, and the jumble might vanish entirely.

      In the paper: Superposition as a Phase Change; The Strategic Picture: Additional Considerations.

      Deeper stillWork out a boundary by hand

      Take the simplest version of each strategy and let p be how often each concept shows up (1 − S). A concept's strength is a random number between 0 and 1, so its average squared distance from its mean (its variance) is V = p/3 − p²/4.

      Ignore B: the model can only guess B's average, so it loses B's variance, weighted by B's importance r: error = r·V.

      See-saw (arrows +1 and −1, no offsets): if only one concept is present, the filter rebuilds it perfectly. If both are present (probability p²), each one's rebuilt value is reduced by the other's. The average squared loss works out to 1/6 for each, so error = p²(1 + r)/6.

      Set them equal and solve: the see-saw beats ignoring B whenever

      p < 4r / (2 + 5r)

      For equally important concepts (r = 1), that's p < 4/7: they can share once each shows up less than 57% of the time. For r = 0.1 it's p < 16%. The live diagram lets every strategy also tune its lengths and offsets, which favours sharing a little more, so its boundary sits slightly to the left of this hand-made one.

      Deeper stillWhen one concept is different

      Real concepts aren't uniform. The paper tried the gentlest change: a pentagon of five concepts, with one of them made more or less rare than the rest. As it gets more common, the other four move away to give it room. As it gets rarer, they crowd in. Push far enough and the pentagon suddenly snaps into two see-saws, with the rare concept dropped. The authors' reading: non-uniform superposition may often be "a deformation of uniform superposition", plus jumps between the uniform shapes.

      A practical note they leave for anyone repeating this: training these tiny 2D models is genuinely hard for gradient descent, and clean results needed several runs per setting, keeping the best. That's why our toys train several copies at once.

      In the paper: Non-Uniform Superposition: Perturbing a Single Feature.

      Who sits with whom

      So far every concept came and went independently. Real concepts don't. "Lawyer" and "court" turn up in the same sentences. "Guilty" and "not guilty" never describe the same verdict. Does the model notice?

      That merging deserves a second look. When "lawyer" and "court" always arrive together and space is tight, the model stops storing them separately. It keeps one arrow pointing between them, a single "legal-setting-ness" direction, and loses the ability to tell them apart. It's like writing one headnote that summarises two cases that are always cited together: cheaper to store, and fine until someone needs to know which case said what.

      Go deeperThe order of preferences

      The paper builds two kinds of related sets. In a correlated set, one coin flip decides whether the whole set is present, so its members always appear together (with independent strengths). In an anticorrelated set, at most one member is active at a time.

      For correlated concepts, the model's preferences run in order. First choice: put them at right angles, in separate factors with no interference between them. If that's impossible: keep them close together, accepting positive interference rather than negative. If there's no room at all: collapse them into their principal component, the blended direction (a + b)/√2, and give up the difference between them. In the paper's six-concept experiment, three correlated pairs form a hexagon with partners side by side when very rare, then "progressively 'collapse'" into their principal components as they get more common.

      Anticorrelated concepts go the other way: they prefer to interfere, ideally as exact opposites, because they never need the desk at the same time. That's the see-saw again, and the paper lists "can superposition pack features more efficiently if they are anti-correlated?" among its open questions.

      Two consequences the authors draw. In bigger models, correlated concepts formed a local almost-orthogonal basis: nearly no superposition inside a group of concepts that appear together, even though the model as a whole is packed. And which concepts share a neuron isn't random: it's shaped by which concepts co-occur. They also see the merging as a sign that two classic strategies trade off: superposition wins when concepts are rare, principal component analysis when they're correlated, and real models probably mix both.

      In the paper: Correlated and Anticorrelated Features, including Local Almost-Orthogonal Bases and Collapsing of Correlated Features.

      Ignoring the whispers

      Sharing has a cost. In the pentagon, the arrows aren't at right angles, so whenever one concept is active, its neighbours receive a faint, false signal: a whisper. The model handles this with a simple rule, built from the bias and the ReLU filter: ignore anything quieter than a certain level.

      And now the jumbled neuron from Scene 1 makes sense. If many concepts share the space, a single neuron's direction catches a little of several of them. It isn't confused. It's a shared desk.

      Go deeperReal neurons, and computing while crowded

      The pentagon model has no special neuron directions: rotate the whole picture and nothing changes. To ask about neurons, the paper added a ReLU in the middle too, which makes each hidden direction special (a privileged basis). With 10 concepts and 5 neurons, it saw neurons go from monosemantic (one concept each) to polysemantic as concepts got rarer, often with both kinds in the same model. Real networks show that same mixture.

      Storage is one thing; doing work is another. The paper's boldest toy asks the model to compute something: the absolute value of each input (turn −0.7 into 0.7). A ReLU network can do that with two neurons per concept, one for the positive side and one for the negative:

      With 100 concepts and only 40 neurons, the trained model still computed absolute values once the inputs were sparse enough, by doing the work in superposition. The authors call this notable because it shows networks "can perform computation even on data that is represented in superposition".

      Its neurons looked eerily familiar. Many had one primary concept with a large weight plus a few secondary ones with small weights. Watch such a neuron's strongest activations and it looks clean; look at its weaker ones and it's a jumble. That's exactly what researchers had reported about neurons in real language models.       In the paper: Superposition in a Privileged Basis; Computation in Superposition.

      Deeper stillA lopsided trick

      Looking closely at one absolute-value model, the authors found a repeated pattern they call asymmetric superposition. Normally two concepts sharing a neuron go in with equal strength. Here one goes in big (say ×2) and the other small (×½), and the output weights are the reciprocals. The result: the big one interferes heavily with the small one, but not the other way round. A second neuron then strongly suppresses the small concept in exactly the cases where that interference would hurt, turning harmful positive interference into harmless negative interference.

      In the paper: Computation in Superposition: The Asymmetric Superposition Motif.

      Scene 9

      Why a crowded office is a safety problem

      Suppose you want to certify that a contract contains no clause creating some liability. You can do it, in principle, by reading every clause. Now imagine the clauses were written on top of one another, thousands to a page. That's what superposition does to a model.

      The authors' deepest motivation was safety. They would like, one day, to be able to say a model will never do certain things, such as deliberately deceive. One promising route is to list every concept a model has and check how each is used: in their words, "a universal quantifier over the fundamental units of neural network computation". With one concept per neuron, that list is just the list of neurons. Superposition takes that shortcut away.

      Careful, though: finding a "deception" concept wouldn't by itself be alarming. A lawyer understands fraud without committing it, and a model that understands the world will represent unsavoury things. The paper makes this point itself: the useful claims are about how such concepts are wired into the model's behaviour.

      If you know crypto

      A Bloom filter is a close cousin of superposition. It records which of many possible items are in a set using far fewer bits than items, by letting items share bits. It works beautifully when the set is sparse, and the price is occasional false positives: interference. Overfill it and the false positives take over. (An analogy for the trade-off, not the same mechanism.)

      Go deeperAdversarial examples, mistakes, and three ways out

      A weak spot built in. In a model without superposition, the read-out for concept 1 listens to concept 1 alone. With superposition, it also listens a little to many others, through all those small overlaps. That's an opening for an attacker: nudge the input along those overlaps and concept 1 misfires. The paper tested this with small, carefully chosen nudges and found vulnerability "sharply increases as superposition forms (increasing by >3×)", tracking the number of concepts per direction. The authors are "hesitant to speculate" how much this explains adversarial examples in real models, since other good explanations exist.

      Mistakes from collisions? The whispers that get through when rare concepts collide are a natural suspect for some of the odd errors real models make. Treat that as a hypothesis: it follows from the toy's logic, but the paper doesn't demonstrate it in real models.

      Three ways out. The paper sketches three strategies. Build models without superposition: easy in toys (an extra penalty on activity, called L1 regularisation, does it), but it costs performance; models like "mixture of experts", which switch on only part of the network for each input, might reduce the need. Find an overcomplete basis: accept the crowding and work out the roster afterwards, the "sparse coding" or dictionary learning problem. The authors warned it would be "a major engineering challenge" at the scale of real models. Hybrids of the two. Chapter 4 is the story of the second route.

      In the paper: Relationship to Adversarial Robustness; The Strategic Picture of Superposition.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      The authors graded their own findings. They expect some to carry over to real networks: superposition itself, the mix of monosemantic and polysemantic neurons, "perhaps the relationship to adversarial examples". Others, "especially the geometry and learning dynamics results", they called "much more uncertain".

      Known: superposition is real, at least in toys

      Small ReLU networks trained on sparse concepts store more concepts than they have directions. Before this paper, the authors knew of no unambiguous demonstration of this in neural networks.

      Known: the filter is what makes it possible

      A linear model never superposes; it keeps the top concepts, like principal component analysis. Adding one ReLU changes the answer.

      Known: rarity and importance decide, with sharp switches

      Whether a concept is dropped, shared or given its own direction depends on how often it appears and how much it matters, and the switch between those fates behaves like a phase change.

      Known: in toys, neat shapes and sensible company

      Toy models settle into see-saws, triangles, pentagons, tetrahedra and square antiprisms. Co-occurring concepts are kept apart or merged; either/or concepts share see-saws.

      Unknown: how realistic are the toys?

      The paper's predictions match what's seen in real networks, such as polysemantic neurons, more of them in later layers of a vision model, and very polysemantic early neurons in language models. Whether the geometry carries over is open.

      Unknown: can we measure a real model's two numbers?

      The toy says importance and rarity curves decide everything. The authors ask whether anyone can estimate them for a real model, and whether superposition shrinks or grows as models scale.

      Unknown: can we untangle it, or avoid it?

      Is there a statistical test that detects superposition? Can training be pushed across the phase boundary into a regime with none? Or can the crowded desks be read after the fact? In 2022, nobody had done it at scale.

      Unknown: what does the leakage cost?

      Some model errors, and some adversarial weaknesses, might come from concepts whispering into each other. That's a hypothesis, not a result.

      Chapter 4

      Untangling the Desks

      Based on Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning (Bricken, Templeton, Batson, Chen, Jermyn et al., Anthropic, 2023)

      Scene 1

      The detective's problem

      Chapter 3 left us with a crowded office. Concepts share desks, so each neuron responds to a jumble of unrelated things. Remember the neuron that fired for citations, dialogue, web requests and Korean? It was found in this very study.

      The researchers now faced a detective's problem. They could see how busy each desk was at every moment: the neuron readings. What they wanted was the roster: who was actually sitting there.

      A court faces the same problem when reconstructing events. The evidence is incomplete, and many stories could fit it. A sensible judge prefers the account that explains all the evidence with the fewest moving parts. That single principle turns out to be the key.

      Scene 2

      Explain every day with as few people as possible

      Their tool is called dictionary learning, carried out with a sparse autoencoder. It works like a translator with a strict rule:

      1. Expand. Take the neuron readings and translate them into a much longer list of possible concepts, the "dictionary". The model in the paper had 512 neurons, and the dictionary had thousands of entries.

      2. Be stingy. For each moment, use as few dictionary entries as possible. Most entries must stay silent. That's what "sparse" means.

      3. Check your work. Translate back, and make sure the result matches the original readings.

      Train that translator on millions of moments, and each dictionary entry tends to settle on one real concept. Researchers call the entries features.

      You can try it on the crowded office from Chapter 3. Below, five hidden concepts share two desks, arranged in the pentagon you saw the model invent. The autoencoder only sees the dots, which are the desk readings at different moments. It has to discover where the five concepts are.

      Scene 4

      What they found in a real model

      The researchers ran this on a small real language model, with one layer and 512 neurons in its thinker layer. They trained dictionaries of many sizes. In the run they studied most closely, the 512 neurons unpacked into about 4,000 features.

      Many were strikingly clear. One feature responded to Arabic script, another to DNA sequences, another to base64 (a way of encoding computer data as letters and numbers), another to Hebrew. When people were asked to judge how interpretable features and neurons were, the features won by a wide margin.

      Three more findings made the result convincing:

      They're real causes, not just labels. Artificially switching a feature on changes what the model writes. Turn up the base64 feature, and the model starts producing base64 text.

      They show up again and again. Train a second model from scratch, and very similar features appear in it. The researchers called this universality.

      The features explain most of what the model does. Swapping the neuron readings for the autoencoder's translation kept most of the model's performance, though not all of it.

      The fine print

      The translation isn't perfect. Some of what the model does slips through the dictionary unexplained, and some features are hard to put into words. And this was a tiny one-layer model. Whether the method would work on a big, modern model was the obvious open question.

      Scene 5

      Zooming in and out

      One more surprise. The size of the dictionary changes what you find. A small dictionary gives broad concepts. A bigger one splits them into finer ones, like zooming in on a map. The paper called this feature splitting, and saw whole families of related features appear as dictionaries grew.

      It's a lot like how a body of law gets organised. Here's an invented example to show the shape of the idea. It's an illustration, not a result from the paper.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: the jumble can be untangled

      A sparse autoencoder can unpack polysemantic neurons into many features that each mean one thing.

      Known: features are real and recurring

      Switching a feature on changes the model's behaviour, and similar features appear in separately trained models.

      Known: the zoom level is a choice

      Bigger dictionaries split broad features into finer ones, in families.

      Unknown: does it work on big models?

      This was a one-layer model with 512 neurons. Modern models are thousands of times larger.

      Unknown: what slips through?

      Part of the model's behaviour isn't captured by the features. Nobody yet knew how much that leftover mattered.

      Unknown: how do features connect?

      Finding the concepts is step one. Seeing how they combine into reasoning is a separate, harder step.

      Chapter 5

      The Golden Gate Bridge

      Based on Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet (Templeton, Conerly, Marcus, Lindsey, Bricken et al., Anthropic, 2024)

      Scene 1

      From a toy to the real thing

      Chapter 4's dictionary worked on a tiny model with one layer and 512 neurons. Sceptics had a fair question: would it survive contact with a real, large model? Or would big models turn out to be an unreadable mess?

      In May 2024, the team tried it on Claude 3 Sonnet, one of Anthropic's production models at the time. They pointed their dictionary at the case file halfway through the model, where the clerks are in the middle of their work, and trained dictionaries of three sizes: 1 million, 4 million and 34 million features.

      Scene 2

      A bridge in every language

      It worked. The dictionaries were full of clear, single-meaning features, and many were far more abstract than Chapter 4's.

      The star was a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge. It switched on when text mentioned the bridge in English, and also in Japanese, Chinese, Greek, Vietnamese, Russian and more. It even switched on for pictures of the bridge, although the dictionary had only ever been trained on text. The model wasn't storing the words "Golden Gate Bridge". It was storing the idea.

      Turning the dial

      Finding a feature is one thing. Proving it matters is another. So the researchers clamped features: they held one at a high value in the case file, at every word, so every later clerk read a file shouting about it.

      Clamp the Golden Gate Bridge feature, and the model couldn't stop talking about it. Ask for a recipe, and the bridge would wander in. Ask what it looked like, and it would say it was the bridge. Anthropic briefly let the public try it as "Golden Gate Claude". It was a vivid demonstration that a feature isn't just a label someone stuck on: it's part of how the model thinks, and changing it changes the model.

        Scene 4

        Features that matter for safety

        The paper looked deliberately for features relevant to safety, and found them. There were features for deception, for flattery, for secrecy, for bias, for power-seeking, and for dangerous subjects such as weapons. Turning some of them up visibly changed the model's behaviour.

        That opened an exciting possibility. If you can see these concepts switching on inside a model, you might be able to check what it's doing, not just trust what it says. For anyone who thinks about audit, compliance or liability, that's a big deal.

        Knowing about something isn't doing it

        The authors stressed that a deception feature doesn't make a model deceptive. A model can represent the idea of deception, just as a lawyer understands fraud without committing it, and anyone who has read a crime novel can picture a lie. What matters is when and how the feature gets used. That question needs more than a dictionary, and it's the subject of the next chapter.

        Scene 5

        What 34 million features still miss

        Even the biggest dictionary didn't catch everything. Common concepts got features early, but rarer ones only appeared in the larger dictionaries, and the researchers judged that many concepts the model knows were still missing at 34 million. Part of the model's activity also slipped through unexplained.

        And a dictionary is a list of ingredients, not a recipe. It tells you which concepts are present, but not how the model combines them to reach an answer.

        The end of the chapter

        What we know, and what we don't

        Known: the method scales

        Dictionary learning found millions of readable features in a large production model.

        Known: features are abstract

        The same feature can respond across languages, and even to images, so it stores the idea rather than the words.

        Known: features steer behaviour

        Clamping a feature changes what the model says and does, from bridge obsession to flattery.

        Unknown: how much is still missing?

        Many concepts didn't have a feature even at 34 million, and finding them all may take far bigger dictionaries.

        Unknown: can this be used for safety checks?

        Safety-relevant features exist. Turning that into reliable monitoring was, and is, unfinished work.

        Unknown: how do features combine?

        A list of concepts isn't an explanation. Tracing how they connect into reasoning was the next challenge.

        Chapter 6

        Watching It Think

        Based on On the Biology of a Large Language Model (Lindsey, Gurnee, Ameisen et al., Anthropic, 2025) and its companion Circuit Tracing (Ameisen, Lindsey, Pearce et al., 2025)

        Scene 1

        From ingredients to recipes

        Chapter 5 gave us a dictionary: millions of concepts a model can hold. But a list of ingredients isn't a recipe. In 2025, the team set out to watch the concepts connect, step by step, as a model works out an answer.

        Their method had two parts. First, they built a stand-in for the model, a replacement model, where the hard-to-read thinker clerks are swapped for dictionary-based versions whose every note is a readable feature. Second, for a given question, they traced which features caused which, producing a map of the model's reasoning called an attribution graph.

        A stand-in could be misleading, so they checked every story against the real model by intervening: switching a feature off, or forcing one on, and seeing whether the answer changed the way the graph predicted. Lawyers will recognise this as the but-for test of causation. But for this concept, would the answer have been different?

        They studied Claude 3.5 Haiku, a production model, and reported a whole series of case studies. Here are some of the most striking.

        Scene 2

        Two hops: Dallas, Texas, Austin

        Ask: "The capital of the state containing Dallas is…". Answering takes two steps: work out that Dallas is in Texas, then recall that the capital of Texas is Austin. Does the model really take both steps, or has it just memorised "Dallas … capital → Austin"?

        The graph showed both steps. A "Texas" feature lit up in the middle of the model, even though the word "Texas" appears nowhere in the question, and it combined with a "say a capital" feature to produce "Austin". There was also a weaker shortcut straight from Dallas to Austin. Then came the but-for test: swap the Texas feature for California, and the model answered Sacramento.

        Scene 3

        Planning the rhyme

        Language models write one word at a time, so you might expect them to improvise, choosing each word only when they get to it. Poetry was a good test. To write a rhyming couplet, do you pick the rhyme first, or hope you land on one?

        The graphs showed planning. At the end of the first line, before writing a single word of the second, the model had already activated features for candidate rhyming words. Then it built the whole line to arrive there. The researchers proved it with interventions: suppress the planned word, and the model rewrote the line to end on a different rhyme. Inject an unrelated word, and it wrote a new line that led to that word instead.

        Scene 4

        Maths in two lanes, and the story it tells

        How does a language model add 36 and 59? It was never taught the school method directly. The graph showed it running two lanes in parallel. One lane makes a rough estimate of the size of the answer, "somewhere in the low nineties". The other works out only the last digit exactly: 6 + 9 ends in 5. Put together, the answer must be 95.

        Then the researchers asked the model how it had done the sum. It described the school method: add the ones, carry the one, add the tens. That isn't what its circuits did. It was repeating the explanation people usually write, not reporting its own process.

        Why this matters beyond maths

        The paper found related cases where the model's written-out reasoning didn't match its internal process. In one, given a hard problem along with a hint about the answer, it worked backwards from the hint and wrote reasoning to fit, much like an advocate building an argument for a conclusion already chosen. A model's explanation of itself is evidence, not proof. Being able to look inside is one way to check it.

        Scene 6

        Knowing when it doesn't know

        Why does a model sometimes make things up? The graphs suggested a surprising answer. The model's default is a circuit that says "I can't answer that". When it recognises a familiar name, a "known entity" feature switches that default off, and it answers.

        That's sensible, like a careful witness who says "I don't recall" unless sure. But it can misfire. If a name feels familiar without the model actually knowing the facts, the default gets switched off anyway, and the model fills the gap with something plausible. That's one route to a hallucination.

        Scene 7

        How much can we really see?

        The authors were frank about the limits. Their method gave a satisfying explanation for only about a quarter of the prompts they tried. The replacement model is an approximation, so some of the real model's work slips through it. Parts of the messengers' work were hard to capture. And reading even one graph took hours of careful human effort.

        Their own comparison was to early biology: the first microscopes, revealing cells for the first time. It's real seeing, but of a small part of a very large organism.

        Known: models take real intermediate steps

        Known: models plan ahead

        Everything is seen through a replacement model. Interventions check it, but gaps remain.

        The biggest open question

        Is it truth, or just what people say?

        Since 2022, researchers outside the Transformer Circuits team have found what look like truth directions inside models: a direction that separates statements the model treats as true from ones it treats as false. If that's real, you could read what a model believes directly, instead of trusting what it says.

        But there's a trap, and lawyers will spot it immediately. Models learn from human writing, which is full of popular myths. So is the direction tracking what is true, or what most people say is true? Researchers call the challenge of reliably getting at what a model really knows eliciting latent knowledge.

        The toy below shows the trap. Imagine a model that stores two things about every statement: whether it's actually true (across) and how widely it's repeated (up). We train a simple "truth detector", called a probe, by showing it labelled examples. Everything depends on who wrote the labels.

        Train a truth detector

        Real calculation · imagined model

        The probe is real: it finds the direction that best separates the two groups of labels (the difference between their averages) and draws a dividing line. The "model" is imagined: we placed each statement by hand on its truth and popularity. Try this: switch between the two kinds of labels. With crowd labels, the detector learns popularity, so it calls popular myths true and little-known facts false, and it still looks like a truth detector.

        In a real model, nobody gets to see the axes. That's why this problem is so hard. Interpretability may help: if you can see which features a probe is relying on, you can tell "true" from "popular". That's the kind of forensic examination the earlier chapters made possible.

        Looking ahead

        The blank spaces

        Old maps marked unexplored land with blank space. Here are the biggest blank spaces on the map of AI, as the field saw them at the end of this story.

        From Chapter 6

        The other three quarters

        Today's tools explain a model's reasoning for only some prompts, and each explanation takes hours. Reading a model routinely, at scale, needs far more automation.

        From Chapters 4 and 5

        What the dictionaries miss

        Part of every model's activity slips through even the biggest dictionaries. Nobody yet knows how much of what matters lives there.

        A bold hypothesis

        Do all models draw the same map?

        A 2024 paper argued that very different models, trained on text or on images, are converging on similar internal geometry as they grow. If so, the map may reflect the world itself, not just our words. It's still debated.

        A moving target

        Models that keep learning

        Today's models are frozen after training. Models that update themselves as they work would be more useful, and much harder to audit: yesterday's check might not hold today.

        The highest stakes

        AI that improves AI

        Models already help write the code and data used to train new models. If that loop speeds up, checking what each change did becomes essential, like auditing an upgradeable smart contract before every upgrade.

        New senses

        Agents that test the world

        Models learned from our words. Agents can run code, search and experiment, checking ideas against reality. That's the likeliest route to models discovering things we don't know, and another reason to see what they're thinking.

        Why it matters

        Testimony and forensics

        A court never relies on a witness's account alone. It tests testimony against physical evidence. For AI, the model's own explanation is the testimony, and Chapter 6 showed it can be sincere and still wrong. Interpretability is the forensic lab: a way to check the account against what actually happened inside.

        That matters for anyone who has to answer for an AI system's decisions: the people who build it, the people who deploy it, the regulators who oversee it, and the judges who may one day have to decide who was responsible. "The model said so" is not a reason. Being able to look inside is how it might become one.

        Ten years ago, nobody could say what happened inside these systems. Today we can trace some of their thoughts, step by step, and change them on purpose. Most of the map is still blank. But the blank spaces now have edges, and people are filling them in.
