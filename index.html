Prologue

Words Become Arrows

2013 to 2020. Based on word2vec (Mikolov et al., Google, 2013), Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (Kaplan et al., OpenAI, 2020) and Zoom In (Olah et al., 2020). Links to all three are at the end of the chapter.

Scene 1

What does a word mean, to a computer?

To a computer, a word is just a row of letters. "Cat" and "kitten" look nothing alike, and "cat" and "car" look almost the same. Spelling tells you very little about meaning.

The way out came from a 1957 idea by the linguist J. R. Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." You learn what a new word means by seeing where it turns up. If a word keeps appearing near "crown", "throne" and "palace", you can guess a lot about it without a dictionary.

So describe every word by its neighbours. Then words with similar neighbours get similar descriptions, and you can draw them as points on a map. Words that keep similar company land close together.

    Scene 3

    2013: the arrow that means something

    In 2013, a team at Google led by Tomas Mikolov released word2vec. It did the same thing as the toy above, on a far bigger scale: billions of words of news text, and a few hundred directions instead of two. (Word2vec learns by predicting a word's neighbours rather than by counting them, but researchers later showed the two approaches are closely related.)

    Then they noticed something nobody had designed. Take the point for king, subtract man, add woman, and you land right next to queen. The step from "man" to "woman" is an arrow, and the same arrow leads from "king" to "queen", from "uncle" to "aunt", from "actor" to "actress".

    Other arrows turned up too. One led from countries to their capitals, another from a verb to its past tense. Meaning wasn't just stored as points. Directions meant things. That single idea runs through every chapter of this book.

    Two fine-print details

    The famous example is one of the neatest. Many analogies work less cleanly, and the standard test quietly leaves out the three words you started with. Without that rule, the nearest word to king − man + woman is often just "king".

    The arrows learned our prejudices too. A 2016 paper showed the same arithmetic completed "man is to computer programmer as woman is to…" with "homemaker". The map reflects the company words keep in human writing, stereotypes included. It was an early warning that these systems absorb what's in their training text, good and bad.

    Scene 4

    From word maps to word machines

    Word2vec gave every word one fixed point. But "bank" means something different in "river bank" and "bank account". A fixed map can't tell them apart.

    In 2017, another Google team introduced the transformer, in a paper called Attention Is All You Need. A transformer reads a whole passage at once and builds a fresh arrow for every word in its context, so the two banks end up in different places. It's the design behind nearly every chatbot today, and the subject of Chapter 1.

    Between 2018 and 2020, OpenAI trained a series of transformers called GPT, GPT-2 and GPT-3, each far bigger than the last. All of them had a single job: read some text and predict the next word. And each one was surprisingly better than the one before.

    Scene 5

    2020: bigger is predictably better

    In 2020, a team at OpenAI led by Jared Kaplan measured exactly how much better. They trained hundreds of models of different sizes and found something rare in science: a near-perfect pattern. Every time you multiply the computing power by ten, the model's error drops by about the same fraction. That held across a huge range, from tiny models to the biggest they could build.

    This pattern is called a scaling law. It's the reason AI companies now spend billions on bigger models with confidence. They can predict roughly how good a model will be before they build it.

    Scene 6

    Grown, not built

    So by 2020, people knew the recipe: a transformer, a mountain of text, the goal of predicting the next word, and a rule that nudges billions of numbers whenever a guess is wrong. They could even predict how good the result would be.

    What they didn't know was what had grown inside. Nobody wrote the rules the model follows. They emerged during training, in a space of thousands of unlabelled directions.

    A small group of researchers led by Chris Olah had already started looking. They took models that recognise images and studied them neuron by neuron. They found neurons that detect curves, and saw how they connect. In their 2020 article Zoom In, they showed a "car detector" assembled from simpler parts: windows near the top, a car body in the middle, wheels at the bottom.

    In 2021, that group began asking the same question of transformers, the models that read and write language. They published their findings as a running series of articles called the Transformer Circuits Thread. This book follows that series, one discovery at a time.

    The end of the prologue

    What we know, and what we don't

    Known: you can know a word by its company

    Describing words by their neighbours gives a map where similar meanings sit close together.

    Known: directions carry meaning

    Steps through the map, like man to woman or country to capital, mean the same thing wherever you take them.

    Known: scale brings predictable gains

    More compute, data and size reduce a model's error along a smooth, predictable curve.

    Unknown: what grows inside?

    In 2020, nobody could explain how a big language model actually produces its answers.

    Unknown: why does scaling work so well?

    The curve was measured, not explained. Why it holds, and how far it will keep holding, are still debated.

    Unknown: are models made of readable parts?

    Circuits had been found in image models. Whether language models could be read the same way was the open bet.

    Chapter 1

    The Clerks and the Case File

    Based on A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (Elhage, Nanda, Olsson et al., Anthropic, 2021)

    The Prologue ended with a bet: that language models are made of parts we can understand. In late 2021, the first big article in the Transformer Circuits series set out to test it. The researchers began with the simplest transformers they could build, ones with zero, one or two layers, and tried to explain exactly what each one does. To keep things simple, they also removed one kind of part, which we'll meet below.

    To follow along, picture a courthouse.

    Scene 1

    One case file per word

    When a transformer reads your sentence, every word gets its own case file. The files are lined up on a long table, in order. At first each file holds very little: which word it is, and where it sits in the sentence.

    Then clerks work through the files, one row of clerks per layer. Each clerk reads, then adds notes. Clerks almost never throw notes away, so the file keeps growing. Researchers call this growing file the residual stream.

    There are two kinds of clerk:

    Messengers look across at earlier files and copy useful notes into the one they're working on. Researchers call them attention heads. They are the only way information travels between words.

    Thinkers work on one file alone, mulling over what's already in it and adding conclusions. These are the MLP layers. They're the part this paper set aside to keep things simple.

    When the clerks are finished, someone reads the last file and uses it to guess the next word. Step through a sketch of that below.

    The case files for "Ms Okafor … thanked Ms"

    A sketch

    This is an illustration, not a real model's notes. Real notes aren't words: they're arrows in a space with thousands of directions, as in the Prologue. The structure is real, though: one file per word, messengers moving notes between files, thinkers working within a file, and the last file used for the guess.

    Scene 2

    Zero clerks: the one-word guesser

    Start with the simplest case: a transformer with no clerks at all. The last file holds only one note, the current word. So the model can only learn "after this word, that word usually comes next." Nothing earlier in the sentence can reach it.

    The paper showed that's exactly what a zero-layer transformer learns: a table of which word tends to follow which. Here's a real one, learned from a 25-sentence courtroom story. Pick a word to see what it guesses comes next.

      Scene 3

      The messenger: where to look, and what to copy

      Give the model one layer of messengers. Now, while working on the last file, a messenger can look back at every earlier file and decide which ones are worth copying from.

      The paper's key insight was that each messenger does two separate jobs, and you can study each on its own:

      Where to look. The current file asks a question, such as "is there a name earlier on?". Every earlier file carries a label that says what it holds. Where question and label match, the messenger pays attention. Researchers call this the QK circuit, for "query" and "key".

      What to copy. Having looked, the messenger writes something into the current file. Often it simply copies the word it found, making that word more likely to come next. This is the OV circuit, for "output" and "value".

      A one-layer model is full of messengers like this. The paper found they learn patterns of the form "A … B → C": when you see A, look back for B, and predict C. One real example was "keep … in → mind" and "keep … at → bay".

      A bug that shows how it works

      Because "where to look" and "what to copy" are separate, these patterns get mixed up. A messenger that learned "keep … in → mind" and "keep … at → bay" also nudges the model towards "keep … in → bay" and "keep … at → mind". It can't remember which pairs belong together. The paper found exactly this kind of mistake, which was good evidence that the explanation was right.

      Scene 4

      Every clerk casts a vote

      Here's the other big insight. Because clerks only ever add notes, the final guess is simply the sum of every clerk's contribution. So you can take a model apart: switch one clerk off, and see what it was adding.

      Below is a tiny real model with two contributors. The one-word guesser from Scene 2 is the direct path. A messenger looks back for names and nouns and copies them forward. Its "where to look" and "what to copy" settings are a handful of numbers we set by hand, but the attention maths is the real thing.

The messenger finds both, but it can't tell which name went with "ms", so it splits its vote between them, and the old habit "reyes" sneaks into first place. A one-layer model can't do better. Fixing that takes a second layer.

      Scene 5

      Two layers: clerks who read each other's notes

      With two layers, something new becomes possible. A second-row messenger can read notes that a first-row messenger left behind, and use them to decide where to look. The paper called this composition.

      Here's how that fixes the mix-up above. A first-row messenger in every file writes down "the word before me was…". Now the file for "okafor" carries the note "the word before me was ms". When a second-row messenger working on a later "ms" asks "which file came right after an earlier ms?", that note answers it, and it copies "okafor".

      That two-clerk team is called an induction head. The paper found induction heads in its two-layer models, and suspected they mattered far more than their simple job suggests. Chapter 2 is the story of finding out how much.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: the case file is a shared workspace

      Each word's file collects notes from every layer, and the final guess is read from the last file.

      Known: messengers do two separable jobs

      Where to look and what to copy can be studied one at a time, and small models can be explained this way exactly.

      Known: contributions add up

      Because clerks add rather than overwrite, you can split a guess into each clerk's vote and switch clerks off to test them.

      Unknown: the thinkers

      The paper left out the MLP "thinker" layers, where much of a real model's work happens. They're much harder to read, for reasons Chapter 3 explains.

      Unknown: does this scale?

      Exact explanations worked for one and two layers. Real models have dozens of layers and thousands of clerks.

      Unknown: how much do induction heads matter?

      They were a neat find in tiny models. Whether they were central to big models was the next question.

      Chapter 2

      The Copycat Trick

      Based on In-context Learning and Induction Heads (Olsson, Elhage, Nanda et al., Anthropic, 2022)

      Scene 1

      Learning without changing

      Here's a puzzle. Once a model has finished training, its billions of numbers are frozen. Nothing about it changes while you talk to it. And yet it clearly learns from what you tell it.

      Lawyers do this with every contract. Write "Northwind Logistics Ltd (the 'Supplier')" on page one, and from then on a good reader knows exactly who "the Supplier" is. A language model does the same thing. Introduce a made-up name, a new term or an odd format, and a few lines later it's using it correctly.

      Researchers call this in-context learning: learning from the text in front of you, rather than from training. You can measure it. A model guesses the 500th word of a document much better than the 50th, because by then it has 450 more words of context to learn from.

      The 2022 paper asked: which clerks make this possible? Their answer was the induction heads we met at the end of Chapter 1.

      Scene 2

      The copycat rule

      An induction head follows one simple rule:

      If you've seen A followed by B before, then the next time you see A, guess B.

      That sounds almost too simple to matter. But it's exactly the trick you need for "the Supplier", for names, for repeated phrases, and for any pattern that appears earlier in the same text.

      The researchers' favourite test makes the point vividly. Take a list of random words, so there's no meaning or grammar to lean on, and repeat it. The first time through, nothing can predict what comes next. The second time through, a model with induction heads gets almost every word right.

      Scene 3

      Two clerks, one trick

      Remember the mix-up at the end of Chapter 1? In "mr osei and ms novak spoke then the judge thanked ms", a single messenger found both names but couldn't tell which one belonged to "ms".

      An induction head is a team of two clerks:

      Clerk 1, in an early row, works on every file and writes down one fact: "the word just before me was…". So the file for "novak" gets the note "before me: ms".

      Clerk 2, in a later row, works on the last file, "ms". It asks: "which earlier file has the note before me: ms?" That's the file for "novak", so it copies "novak" forward.

      Clerk 2 can only ask that question because Clerk 1 left the note. This is the composition from Chapter 1: one clerk's notes changing where another clerk looks.

      Scene 4

      The bump in the curve

      Here's what made the paper famous. When you train a model, its error usually falls smoothly. But the researchers spotted a small bump early in training, where progress stalls and then lurches forward.

      When they looked closely, three things happened at the same moment, in model after model:

      1. Induction heads suddenly formed. 2. The model's in-context learning jumped: it got much better at using earlier text. 3. The bump appeared in the training curve.

      And models with only one layer, which can't build the two-clerk team, showed no bump and no jump. Scrub through training below to see the pattern.

      Scene 5

      More than copying

      In bigger models, the researchers found induction heads doing looser versions of the trick. Instead of "A followed by B", it becomes "something like A, followed by something like B". Some heads helped with translation, lining up a word in one language with its partner in another. Others continued patterns that were similar in meaning rather than identical in spelling.

      That led to the paper's bold suggestion: induction heads might be the mechanism behind most of the in-context learning in large models, not just the literal copying.

      How strong is the evidence?

      The authors were careful to grade their own claims. In small models with no thinker layers, the evidence was causal: switch the induction heads off, and in-context learning largely disappears. They even changed the architecture so a one-layer model could form induction heads, and the bump moved with them.

      In large models, the evidence was mostly correlational: induction heads and in-context learning arrive together, but that doesn't prove one causes the other. As a lawyer might put it, a strong circumstantial case, not a confession.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: models learn from context

      Without changing their weights, models get better the more of a document they read.

      Known: induction heads copy patterns

      A two-clerk team carries out "A then B, so after A guess B", using one clerk's notes to direct another's attention.

      Known: they arrive suddenly

      Induction heads, better in-context learning and a bump in the training curve appear together, early in training.

      Unknown: is this most of it?

      Whether induction heads explain most in-context learning in large models was a well-argued hypothesis, not a proof.

      Unknown: what about the thinkers?

      These results were cleanest in models without MLP "thinker" layers. Real models are full of them.

      Unknown: why are the thinkers so hard to read?

      Their neurons kept responding to unrelated things at once. Explaining that puzzle is the next chapter.

      Chapter 3

      The Crowded Office

      Based on Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage et al., Anthropic, 2022)

      Here's a reminder of the characters you'll meet in every chapter. Inside a language model, every idea it can think about is stored as an arrow: a direction in a vast space. The space is the model's private filing system. Researchers call it latent space, which just means "hidden space", because nobody labelled any of its directions.

      The case file

      What the model is thinking about right now. It's passed from clerk to clerk as the model reads your words.

      The clerks

      The model's layers. Each one reads the file, adds a few notes, and passes it on.

      The concepts

      Ideas like "French", "a legal term" or "octopus". There are millions of them.

      The desks

      The directions available to store concepts in. There are only a few thousand.

      Scene 1

      The neuron that couldn't make up its mind

      Early researchers had a hopeful plan. A model is made of tiny parts called neurons. If each neuron stood for one idea, you could read a model like a dictionary: here's the "French" neuron, here's the "angry" neuron.

      Sometimes it worked. But again and again, they found neurons like this one, which lights up for things that have nothing to do with each other.

      Meet one jumbled neuron

      Try it

      This pattern is a real one: a 2023 Anthropic study found a single neuron in a small language model that responded to academic citations, English dialogue, web requests and Korean text. Researchers call neurons like this polysemantic, meaning "many meanings". For years, they were one of the field's biggest puzzles.

      Scene 2

      Too many people, too few desks

      Here's the problem. A model knows about millions of concepts, but it only has a few thousand directions to store them in. That's like an office with a thousand employees and a dozen desks.

      Offices solve this with hot-desking. If most people only come in occasionally, they can share. The rarer the visits, the less often two people want the same desk on the same day.

Most concepts are rare (you can read a lot of text before anyone mentions octopus anatomy), so they can share space and rarely get in each other's way.

      Scene 3

      A room that's much bigger than it looks

      For a model, a "desk" is a direction. Two concepts stay completely out of each other's way when their arrows are at a right angle. On a flat page, you can only fit two arrows at right angles: one across, one up.

      But a model's space doesn't have two directions. It has thousands. And in a space like that, something strange happens: if you pick arrows completely at random, almost every pair ends up nearly at a right angle. There's room for vastly more "almost separate" concepts than there are directions.

      Scene 4

      Watch a real tiny model learn

      This is the experiment at the heart of the paper. The researchers built the smallest possible model: it has to store 5 concepts in a space with only 2 directions. Then they asked what it does when the concepts are common, and what it does when they're rare.

      The model below isn't an animation. It's a real neural network training live in your browser, using the same setup. Each coloured arrow is where it chose to store one concept. Concept 1 matters most to its score, concept 5 matters least.

      Try this: start at "every time" and wait a few seconds. Then slide towards "very rarely", one notch at a time, and watch the arrows rearrange themselves.

      Here's what you should see, and it's what the researchers saw.

      When concepts are common, the model behaves like a cautious filing clerk. It keeps only the two most important concepts, gives each a private direction at a right angle, and simply gives up on the other three. Sharing would cause too many clashes.

      As concepts get rarer, the model starts squeezing more in. First three. Then four, arranged as two pairs pointing in opposite directions. Finally all five, spread into a perfect pentagon. Nobody told it to make a pentagon. It found that shape on its own, because it's the fairest way to give five rare concepts as much room as possible.

      That squeezing is superposition: more concepts than directions, sharing the space because they seldom appear at the same time.

      Scene 5

      Ignoring the whispers

      Sharing has a cost. When concepts squeeze together, each one leaks a little into its neighbours. Look at the pentagon: the arrows aren't at right angles, so whenever one concept is active, its neighbours get a faint, false signal too.

      The model handles this with a simple rule: ignore anything quieter than a certain level. Faint leakage gets thrown away, and only the strong, real signal gets through. This works only because the concepts are rare. If they all showed up at once, the leaks would pile up into a roar.

      Two more findings

      Concepts that appear together get kept apart. "Lawyer" and "court" turn up in the same sentences all the time, so sharing would mean constant clashes. Models tend to give such pairs separate directions.

      Opposites make perfect desk-mates. Concepts that never appear together can point in exactly opposite directions, like the two ends of a see-saw. Only one can be up at a time. You may have spotted these pairs in the toy model above.

      And now the jumbled neuron from Scene 1 makes sense. A neuron is a single, fixed spot in the model. When many concepts share the space, a single neuron catches a little of several of them. It isn't confused. It's a shared desk.

      The end of the chapter

      What we know, and what we don't

      Known: important, common ideas get their own direction

      When space is tight, models prioritise. The ideas that matter most and come up most often get private directions.

      Known: rare ideas share space

      Superposition lets a model store far more concepts than it has directions, as long as those concepts seldom appear together.

      Known: it makes neat shapes on its own

      In toy models, shared concepts settle into pairs, triangles, pentagons and other tidy geometric arrangements.

      Unknown: how much of this happens in big models?

      The paper studied tiny toy models. Whether giant models use superposition in exactly the same way was an open question in 2022.

      Unknown: can we untangle it?

      If concepts are squeezed together, reading a model means pulling them back apart. In 2022, nobody had shown how to do that at scale.

      Unknown: what does the leakage cost?

      Researchers suspect that some model mistakes come from concepts leaking into each other at the wrong moment. That's still a hypothesis.

      Chapter 4

      Untangling the Desks

      Based on Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning (Bricken, Templeton, Batson, Chen, Jermyn et al., Anthropic, 2023)

      Scene 1

      The detective's problem

      Chapter 3 left us with a crowded office. Concepts share desks, so each neuron responds to a jumble of unrelated things. Remember the neuron that fired for citations, dialogue, web requests and Korean? It was found in this very study.

      The researchers now faced a detective's problem. They could see how busy each desk was at every moment: the neuron readings. What they wanted was the roster: who was actually sitting there.

      A court faces the same problem when reconstructing events. The evidence is incomplete, and many stories could fit it. A sensible judge prefers the account that explains all the evidence with the fewest moving parts. That single principle turns out to be the key.

      Scene 2

      Explain every day with as few people as possible

      Their tool is called dictionary learning, carried out with a sparse autoencoder. It works like a translator with a strict rule:

      1. Expand. Take the neuron readings and translate them into a much longer list of possible concepts, the "dictionary". The model in the paper had 512 neurons, and the dictionary had thousands of entries.

      2. Be stingy. For each moment, use as few dictionary entries as possible. Most entries must stay silent. That's what "sparse" means.

      3. Check your work. Translate back, and make sure the result matches the original readings.

      Train that translator on millions of moments, and each dictionary entry tends to settle on one real concept. Researchers call the entries features.

      Scene 4

      What they found in a real model

      The researchers ran this on a small real language model, with one layer and 512 neurons in its thinker layer. They trained dictionaries of many sizes. In the run they studied most closely, the 512 neurons unpacked into about 4,000 features.

      Many were strikingly clear. One feature responded to Arabic script, another to DNA sequences, another to base64 (a way of encoding computer data as letters and numbers), another to Hebrew. When people were asked to judge how interpretable features and neurons were, the features won by a wide margin.

      Three more findings made the result convincing:

      They're real causes, not just labels. Artificially switching a feature on changes what the model writes. Turn up the base64 feature, and the model starts producing base64 text.

      They show up again and again. Train a second model from scratch, and very similar features appear in it. The researchers called this universality.

      The features explain most of what the model does. Swapping the neuron readings for the autoencoder's translation kept most of the model's performance, though not all of it.

      The fine print

      The translation isn't perfect. Some of what the model does slips through the dictionary unexplained, and some features are hard to put into words. And this was a tiny one-layer model. Whether the method would work on a big, modern model was the obvious open question.

        Scene 1

        From ingredients to recipes

        Chapter 5 gave us a dictionary: millions of concepts a model can hold. But a list of ingredients isn't a recipe. In 2025, the team set out to watch the concepts connect, step by step, as a model works out an answer.

        Their method had two parts. First, they built a stand-in for the model, a replacement model, where the hard-to-read thinker clerks are swapped for dictionary-based versions whose every note is a readable feature. Second, for a given question, they traced which features caused which, producing a map of the model's reasoning called an attribution graph.

        A stand-in could be misleading, so they checked every story against the real model by intervening: switching a feature off, or forcing one on, and seeing whether the answer changed the way the graph predicted. Lawyers will recognise this as the but-for test of causation. But for this concept, would the answer have been different?

        They studied Claude 3.5 Haiku, a production model, and reported a whole series of case studies. Here are some of the most striking.

        The biggest open question

        Is it truth, or just what people say?

        Every trial lawyer knows the witness who repeats what the whole town says. They're sincere. They may even be right. But you can't tell from their confidence whether they saw it themselves or heard it at the pub.

        A language model is trained to continue human writing, and human writing is full of things the whole town says. In 2022 a team at UC Berkeley put the worry bluntly: train a model to imitate people, and they may reproduce errors that humans make; train it to please human raters, and it may produce errors that human evaluators can't detect. So they asked a different question. Forget what the model says. Is there something in its case files that tracks what's actually true?

        Researchers call this eliciting latent knowledge: getting at what a model knows, even when its words don't show it. The simplest tool for the job is a probe, a tiny detector that reads a model's case file and outputs a single verdict, true or false. Everything depends on how you train it.

        Below is a model we've imagined so you can see its insides. It stores two notes about every statement: whether the statement is actually true (across) and how widely repeated it is (up). A real model's case file has thousands of directions and nobody labels them. Here, you get to see the two that matter.

        The detector is real. With labels, it takes the average position of the "true" statements and the average of the "false" ones, points an arrow from one to the other, and draws its dividing line halfway: a difference-of-means probe, the same kind Marks and Tegmark used. With no labels, it finds the direction in which the statements are most spread out (the top principal component) and splits along that; because an unlabelled method can't know which end means "true", we let it peek at the answers to choose the end, as Burns et al. did when scoring their method.         That last setting is the whole puzzle in miniature. A method with no labels has nothing to go on except the shape of the case files. It finds whatever stands out. If truth stands out, wonderful. If "what everyone says" stands out more, it will hand you that, and it will look just as confident.

        Go deeper: how do you find truth with no labels at all? Burns et al., 2022

        Burns and colleagues needed a property that truth has and most other notes in a case file don't. They chose logic. A statement and its opposite can't both be true, and one of them must be. So they built contrast pairs: the same yes-or-no question answered "Yes" and answered "No". Then they searched for a direction in the case file where the two halves of every pair land at opposite ends. They called the method contrast-consistent search (CCS).

        Written the paper's way, with p as the probe's probability that a statement is true, x⁺ the "Yes" version and x⁻ the "No" version, the search rewards two things:

        consistency: p(x⁺) ≈ 1 − p(x⁻)
        confidence: min{ p(x⁺), p(x⁻) } ≈ 0

        In words: the two answers' chances of being true should add up to one, and the probe shouldn't hedge at fifty-fifty. Neither rule works alone. Consistency by itself is satisfied by a probe that says 0.5 to everything, a witness who answers every question with "possibly".

        71.2%CCS accuracy, averaged over 5 models and 10 datasets, using no labels and no model outputs
        67.2%the model's own answers (calibrated zero-shot) on the same questions
        83.7%a probe trained with labels: the ceiling CCS was chasing

        The most telling test was a trap. They put wrong answers to earlier questions at the start of the prompt, inviting the model to play along. In one model (UnifiedQA), the model's spoken answers fell from 80.4% to 70.9% correct. CCS, reading the case files, went from 82.1% to 83.8%. The words were swayed; the hidden signal wasn't. The authors add a footnote of caution: the trick may have worked partly because the odd prompt was unusual, not because the model was "playing along".

        In the paper: section 2.2, Method: Contrast-Consistent Search; section 3.2.2, CCS Is Robust To Misleading Prompts.

        Deeper still: truth is "salient", and the catch that follows

        Burns et al. found that a direction separating true from false is often easy to find. It sometimes works from a single contrast pair, and simply taking the biggest spread of the differences between the two halves of each pair (the method they called CRC) scored 70.1%, almost as well as CCS. Their section title says it plainly: Truth is a Salient Feature.

        That's encouraging, and it's also the trap from the toy. Consistency is a property of any belief held coherently. A town that believes bats are blind also believes "bats are not blind" is false. A consistent popular myth passes the same test as a fact. The authors were candid about the gap: CCS only works if the model evaluates the truth of what it reads, and they didn't test models that were actively lying, because no good test setup existed yet.

        In the paper: section 3.3.3, Truth is a Salient Feature; section 5.1, Limitations and Future Work.

        Go deeper: does the truth direction actually do anything? Marks & Tegmark, 2023

        A year later, Samuel Marks and Max Tegmark went back to basics. Earlier probes had a bad habit: some broke as soon as you added the word "not". So they built clean, boring datasets where nothing varies except truth: "The city of Tokyo is in Japan", "Sixty-one is larger than seventy-four", "The Spanish word 'nariz' does not mean 'giraffe'". Then they looked inside three LLaMA-2 models of 7, 13 and 70 billion parameters.

        Where the verdict lives. By swapping one case file at a time between a true and a false version of a prompt (Chapter 6's but-for test again), they found the truth signal gathered in the case files over the end of the statement, especially the full stop. The model seems to file a summary of the whole sentence there.

        What it looks like. Squash those case files down to two directions, as in the Prologue's word map, and true statements fall on one side and false ones on the other. The pattern sharpens with size. In the 7-billion model, number statements clustered by surface details, such as whether they contained the word "eighty". In the 13-billion model, "larger than" and "smaller than" statements separated in opposite directions. In the 70-billion model they lined up along one shared direction.

        Our toy is their probe. Their favourite detector was the mass-mean probe: θ = μ⁺ − μ⁻, the arrow from the average false statement to the average true one. That is exactly what the toy above computes. They preferred it to the more common logistic regression for a reason Chapter 3 explains: when concepts share space in superposition, logistic regression tilts its arrow to dodge the overlap and can miss the real direction.

        Pushing on it. The decisive test was causal, like clamping the Golden Gate feature in Chapter 5. They added the truth arrow to the case file of a false statement and asked the model whether it was true. Shifts found from city statements and their negations made the 13-billion model call false Spanish translations true, at up to 85% of the strength it gives genuinely true ones (and 97% the other way). Training on one topic changed the verdict on another.

        r = +0.85how closely "probable text" and "true" go together for city statements
        r = −0.63the same, for the negated cities: "Paris is not in France" sounds natural and is false

        Those two numbers are their answer to "is it just tracking likely-sounding text?" Add "not" and plausibility and truth come apart, so a detector that only knew "sounds familiar" would fail. Probes trained on a dataset of merely likely text did worse than chance on such sets. Training on statements and their negations helped the real probes generalise.

        "we focus on simple, uncontroversial statements, and therefore cannot disambiguate truth from closely related features, such as 'commonly believed' or 'verifiable'."Marks & Tegmark, section 7.1

        That sentence is the toy's trap, in the authors' own words. On their datasets, "true" and "believed by everyone" are the same thing, so nothing in the data could pull them apart. The question this scene is named after is still open.

        In the paper: section 3, Localizing truth representations via patching; section 4, Visualizing LLM representations; section 5.1, Challenges with logistic regression, and mass-mean probing; section 6, Causal intervention experiments; appendix A, Scoping of truth.

        So what would separate the two? Here is a hope, and it is only a hope. A popular myth is a weak precedent. It survives on its own, but put it next to the rest of the case law and it clashes. "Goldfish forget in three seconds" sits awkwardly beside everything else the model has read about how fish are trained to respond to feeding times. If a large model is under pressure to keep a thousand related notes coherent, the consistent picture may win out over the merely popular one. Nobody has shown that this happens. It's a reason to test, not a reason to trust.

        A second open question

        Can a model know something nobody knew?

        The sharpest critique of language models has a memorable name. A 2021 paper called them stochastic parrots: systems that stitch together fragments of what they've seen, with no grasp of meaning. If that's all they are, they can never tell us anything new.

        The honest reply starts with a concession. Models do recombine what they've read. But so do we. Most human creativity is recombination, and so is most good lawyering: a novel argument is usually an old principle applied to a new set of facts, or two lines of authority joined for the first time. Recombination can produce something genuinely new. The harder question is how anyone would know it's new, and right.

        The answer that has worked so far is old-fashioned: propose, then check. Let a model suggest ideas, and let something that can't be talked round, a program that tests each idea, decide which ones survive. In 2023 a Google DeepMind team built exactly that, called FunSearch, and pointed it at a puzzle the mathematician Terence Tao had called perhaps my favourite open question: the cap set problem.

        Each round, the proposer takes the best set so far, drops a few dots at random and adds a few at random. The checker tests every triple of dots for a line and rejects any proposal that contains one. Both are real. Try this: tick "skip the checker" and run it. The "record" shoots past 60 dots, and every one of those sets is riddled with lines: confident, impressive and wrong. With the checker on, three dimensions is easy (the best possible is 9). In four, our blind proposer usually stalls at 17 or 18, short of the 20 that's known to be possible.

        Stalling is the interesting part. A checker keeps you honest, but it can't make you clever. Random edits get trapped in sets that can't be improved one dot at a time. FunSearch's trick was to make the proposer smarter: instead of random edits, a language model wrote small programs that build cap sets, the checker scored them, and the best programs were fed back to the model as examples to improve on.

        Go deeper: what propose-then-check has actually found

        FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., Google DeepMind, Nature, 2024). In eight dimensions it found a cap set of 512 dots, larger than any previously known. On the deeper question, how fast the best cap sets grow as dimensions are added, it nudged the best proven lower bound from 2.2180 to 2.2202, which the authors describe as the largest improvement in 20 years. Because FunSearch outputs a program rather than a list of dots, mathematicians could read the code, spot the symmetry it was using, and push the result further by hand. The authors' framing: a frozen language model to provide creative solutions, paired with an evaluator which guards against confabulations.

        AlphaEvolve (Google DeepMind white paper, June 2025) scaled up the same loop. Its most quoted result: a way to multiply two 4×4 complex-valued matrices using 48 multiplications, which the paper calls the first improvement, after 56 years, over Strassen's algorithm in this setting. It also found a faster way to schedule Google's data centres, and sped up the training of the very model that AlphaEvolve runs on. Keep that last one in mind for the blank spaces below.

        And a cautionary tale. In October 2025, researchers at OpenAI announced that GPT-5 had solved ten "open" Erdős problems. The mathematician who maintains the list where they were marked open, Thomas Bloom, called this a dramatic misrepresentation: the model had found references, which solved these problems, that I personally was unaware of. "Open" on his site meant only that he hadn't seen a solution. The model had done something genuinely useful, an excellent literature search, but it hadn't discovered anything. Every lawyer who has drafted a patent knows the lesson: before you claim something is new, search the prior art.

        FunSearch: sections Cap sets and Admissible sets. AlphaEvolve: the abstract. The Erdős episode: reported by eWeek, 20 October 2025.

        New senses

        Shadows on the cave wall

        Notice what the checker did. It was the one part of FunSearch that touched reality. For most of this story, a language model had no senses of its own. Everything it knew came through us: text we wrote about things we had seen. We were its only eyes and ears, and it inherited our blind spots along with our insights.

        Agents change that. A model that can run code, search, query a database or run an experiment can check an idea against the world instead of against the opinion of the crowd. That's the same move a court makes when it orders a site inspection rather than relying on the witnesses' accounts. It's the likeliest route to models finding things out that nobody wrote down, and it's one more reason to want to see what they're thinking while they do it.

        But is there a world in a model's head at all, or only words? In 2024 a team at MIT made a bold claim. They argued that models trained on completely different things, pictures for some and text for others, are drifting towards the same internal map as they get better. They named the idea after Plato's cave, where prisoners see only shadows of real objects on a wall: the Platonic representation hypothesis.

        "Neural networks, trained with different objectives on different data and modalities, are converging to a shared statistical model of reality in their representation spaces."Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola, 2024

        How do you compare two maps drawn in different languages? You can't line up their directions; they don't share any. Instead you ask, for each thing, which other things each model thinks are its closest neighbours, and how often the two lists overlap. Try it on a toy world.

        Forty things exist in a hidden world with ten properties. A "camera" model and a "text" model each see some of those properties. For every thing, we list its 5 nearest neighbours in each model and count the overlap.

        This is the paper's own measure, mutual nearest-neighbour alignment, computed live on a world we invented. 1 means the two models agree perfectly about what resembles what; the grey tick marks what pure chance would give. Try this: slide the shared properties up and watch alignment rise. With private senses on, it climbs from 0.10 to about 0.5 and stops there, because each model knows something the other can't, and that private knowledge reshuffles its idea of who resembles whom. Now untick the private senses. As soon as the two share even one property, alignment jumps to 1.00: with nothing private, they are seeing the same world through the same window.

        Go deeper: the evidence, and the Prologue's word map returns Huh et al., 2024

        The evidence. Across 78 vision models, the ones that did best on a broad set of tasks had the most similar internal maps. In the authors' Tolstoy-flavoured phrase, all strong models are alike, each weak model is weak in its own way. Then the cross-modal test: using Wikipedia images and their captions, they compared language models with vision models. The better a language model was at predicting text, the more its map of the captions resembled a vision model's map of the pictures.

        Why it might happen. They offer three pressures. More tasks leave fewer ways to solve them all. Bigger models are more likely to find the shared best solution. And deep networks lean towards simple fits, like the judge in Chapter 4 who prefers the account with the fewest moving parts.

        The callback. In an idealised world, they show that a family of learning methods ends up with a map where the closeness of two things equals their pointwise mutual information: how much more often they turn up together than chance would predict. That's the quantity the Prologue's word map was built from, live in your browser, eight chapters ago. "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" may be a special case of a much bigger rule.

        In the paper: section 2.2, Alignment increases with scale and performance; section 2.3, Representations are converging across modalities; section 4.2, on contrastive learners and PMI.

        Deeper still: what the authors themselves doubt

        The authors list their own counterexamples, and they're good ones. Different senses carry different information: can text capture a total solar eclipse, or a photo capture "I believe in the freedom of speech"? Their mathematical argument only strictly holds when every sense captures everything about the world, which no real sense does. Specialised systems, like a protein-folder or a lane-follower, may find shortcuts that have little to do with reality. And the field itself may be pushing models towards human-like maps by copying human designs.

        The numbers are humbling too. The language-vision alignment in their main figure rises clearly, but only to 0.16 on a scale where 1 is perfect. The authors leave open whether that's strong agreement plus noise, or poor alignment with major differences left to explain.

        Even if the hypothesis is right, the map is still made of data. A model can only converge on the parts of reality its training data leaves a trace of. A fact nobody ever measured, wrote down or photographed can't be conjured from the shadows. The authors say as much: their hope that scale will reduce hallucination is conditioned on training data that is a sufficiently lossless and diverse set of measurements.

        In the paper: section 6, Counterexamples and limitations, including Lots left to explain; section 5, Scaling may reduce hallucination and bias.

        Looking ahead

        The blank spaces

        Old maps marked unexplored land with blank space. Here are the biggest blank spaces on the map of AI, as they look in late 2026. Each one opens up: some with early findings, some with nothing more than an intuition, clearly labelled.

        From Chapter 6

        The other three quarters

        Attribution graphs gave a satisfying explanation for about a quarter of the prompts tried, and each took hours of expert work. Reading a model routinely needs far more automation.

        Early findings

        In July 2025 Anthropic researchers built AI agents to do the auditing. In a test where a model had been deliberately trained with a hidden goal, a single investigator agent found the root cause 10–13% of the time with realistic tools, and 42% when many agents' findings were pooled. Its success depended on having interpretability tools or a search over training data. Their caveat: the hidden goals were planted by researchers and may not resemble real problems.

        Bricken et al., Building and evaluating alignment auditing agents, Alignment Science Blog, 2025.

        From Chapters 4 and 5

        What the dictionaries miss

        Part of every model's activity slips through even the biggest dictionaries. Nobody yet knows how much of what matters lives there.

        Why it matters here

        A truth probe, an audit or a safety monitor is only as good as the features it can see. If the decisive step of some reasoning sits in the unexplained remainder, a clean-looking graph can be clean and incomplete at the same time, like a ledger that balances because a page is missing.

        A moving target

        Models that keep learning

        Today's models are mostly frozen after training. Models that keep learning as they work would be more useful, and much harder to audit: yesterday's clean bill of health might not hold today.

        Known problem, and an intuition

        Known: neural networks that learn new things in sequence tend to overwrite old skills. It's called catastrophic forgetting, and it has been studied since the late 1980s.

        An intuition, not a finding: superposition may make this worse. In Chapter 3's crowded office, every desk is shared, so adjusting one dial to learn a new concept also nudges every concept that shares its desk. A model that edits itself constantly could disturb things nobody was watching. Nobody has shown how large this effect is in big models.

        The highest stakes

        AI that improves AI

        Models already help write the code and data used to train new models. AlphaEvolve, above, sped up the training of the model it runs on. If that loop tightens, every upgrade needs an audit of what it changed.

        The smart-contract view

        Crypto has lived this problem. An upgradeable smart contract can have its logic replaced after deployment, so users' safety rests entirely on someone checking each new version before it goes live. Tezos went further and made the blockchain self-amending: the protocol itself proposes, votes on and installs its own upgrades. A model that trains its successor is the same design, with weights instead of code, and no readable diff.

        Building that diff is now a research topic. In a December 2024 note, Anthropic researchers compared a model's dictionary features before and after a fine-tune that secretly planted a "sleeper agent" behaviour, and the comparison picked out features tied to the planted behaviour. They described the method as having good sensitivity but not specificity: it found the needle, along with straw that had to be checked by steering.

        Bricken et al., Stage-Wise Model Diffing, Transformer Circuits Thread, December 2024 (a preliminary note).

        The open question of this chapter

        True, or merely believed?

        Truth directions exist in the models tested, and they're causal. Whether they track what is true or what is commonly believed is exactly what current datasets can't separate.

        What would settle it

        Datasets where truth and popularity are deliberately pulled apart, at scale: settled myths, claims that were once consensus and later overturned, and specialist facts that few people repeat. Then the forensic step: interpretability tools that show which features a probe is actually reading.

        New senses

        Agents that test the world

        Models learned from our words. Agents can run code, search and experiment. That's the likeliest route to discoveries nobody has made, and it makes seeing their reasoning more important, not less.

        The open part

        A checker only certifies what it was built to check. FunSearch's evaluator could prove a cap set had no lines; it couldn't judge whether a problem was worth solving. As agents choose their own experiments, deciding what counts as a good check becomes the human's job, and possibly the hardest one.

        Why it matters

        Testimony and forensics

        A court never relies on a witness's account alone. It tests testimony against physical evidence. For AI, the model's own explanation is the testimony, and Chapter 6 showed it can be sincere and still wrong: the model described carrying the one while its circuits did something else entirely. Interpretability is the forensic lab: a way to check the account against what actually happened inside.

        The lab has started examining the witness directly. In October 2025, Jack Lindsey at Anthropic planted concepts in a model's case file, such as an "all caps" direction, and asked whether it noticed anything. The most capable models tested sometimes did, naming the planted concept before it could have shown up in their own words. But only about 20% of the time at best, and the paper is blunt that failures of introspection remain the norm. Its conclusion reads like a brief for this book:

        "the most important role of interpretability research may shift from dissecting the mechanisms underlying models' behavior, to building 'lie detectors' to validate models' own self-reports about these mechanisms."Lindsey, Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models, 2025

        That matters for anyone who has to answer for an AI system's decisions: the people who build it, the people who deploy it, the regulators who oversee it, and the judges who may one day have to decide who was responsible. "The model said so" is not a reason. Being able to look inside is how it might become one.

        Ten years ago, nobody could say what happened inside these systems. Today we can trace some of their thoughts, step by step, change them on purpose, and even catch them disagreeing with their own words. Most of the map is still blank. But the blank spaces now have edges, and people are filling them in.

        The end of the story, for now

        What we know, and what we don't

        Known: simple probes find a truth signal

        In the models tested, a single direction in the case file separates simple true statements from false ones, and it can be found with labels or, often, without.

        Known: the truth direction is causal

        Pushing a LLaMA-2 model's case file along it makes the model call false statements true, even on topics the probe never saw.

        Known: propose-then-check finds new results

        Pairing a language model with a strict evaluator produced verified new mathematics, including larger cap sets and a faster 4×4 matrix method.

        Unknown: truth, or what's believed?

        On current datasets the two coincide, so no one can yet say which one the direction tracks. The coherence argument is a hope.

        Unknown: one map for all models?

        Different models' maps are growing more alike, but alignment is still low, and different senses capture different parts of the world.

        Unknown: auditing a moving target

        Models that keep learning, or help build their successors, need audits of every change. The tools for that are preliminary.
